Multi-agent large language model (LLM) systems commonly assign distinct roles such as Planner, Executor, and Reviewer. In this paper, we investigate what these roles actually change beyond their intended division of labor. We evaluate three open-weight models on the Omni-MATH benchmark and two of them on the SciBench benchmark. Throughout these experiments, we hold the agents, agent topology, call budget, and ground-truth evaluator constant. We find that deleting all role instructions largely eliminates functional specialization, yet the team's first-pass accuracy does not drop. Roles also allocate decision rights. A role-structured Reviewer often withholds approval and forces internal deliberation. Consequently, its team submits answers to the external evaluator less frequently and receives less corrective feedback. In an exploratory comparison where feedback is accessible to both teams, the role-free team recovers from a failed first answer more often across all five actor-domain settings and achieves higher final accuracy. A post hoc analysis reveals that this role-free advantage disappears when comparing the two teams at an equal number of evaluator verdicts. In a second exploratory intervention, run for the gemma-4 model on 400 Omni-MATH problems, we preserve the role prompts but force a submission in every cycle, and the role-structured team's recovery rate then matches that of the role-free team. We conclude that roles govern decision rights and information flow as well as work allocation. Evaluating multi-agent organizations therefore requires looking beyond role fidelity to analyze decision policies, feedback access, and failure recovery.
