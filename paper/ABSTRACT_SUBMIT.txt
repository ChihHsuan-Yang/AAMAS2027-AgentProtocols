Multi-agent LLM systems are specified as role diagrams -- Planner, Executor, Reviewer -- and the diagram is read as a mechanism. We ask when the roles are load-bearing. Holding agents, topology, call budget, evaluator and grading fixed, we delete every role instruction and, separately, enforce the same division of labor at the interface, across three actor models (11,600 frozen trajectories), plus an exploratory post-freeze role-free condition that receives the evaluator's feedback. Deleting the roles destroys the intended division of labor on Omni-MATH -- the rate at which every agent asserts its own final answer rises from 0.000--0.120 to 0.846--0.976 -- while first-pass accuracy does not fall. Roles look load-bearing for repair only against a role-free team whose prompts never carried the evaluator's feedback (gemma-4: 0.222 with roles, 0.051 without). Given the same feedback string, the role-free team recovers more: 0.609 against 0.222 for gemma-4 (89 jointly failed problems recovered only without roles, 4 only with them; exact McNemar p=6.2x10^-22), and reaches higher final accuracy in all five actor--domain cells. Yet when both teams are credited with the same number of verdicts per problem, the role-free advantage disappears: the pooled difference is +0.009 in favor of roles (95% CI -0.014 to +0.033); no robustness check favors the role-free team significantly, and one favors roles. Roles mainly change how often a team asks for feedback. The role-structured Reviewer withholds approval: on Omni-MATH, gemma-4's team submits 1.27 times per failed trajectory against 3.20. Overriding the Reviewer in an exploratory 400-problem run, role prompts unchanged, raises gemma-4's recovery from 0.198 (gated re-run) to 0.613, matching the role-free team's 0.549 on the same problems. Structure is not adaptability: a clean division of labor is not evidence that a team acts on corrective information, and role ablations must hold information channels fixed, not only budgets.
