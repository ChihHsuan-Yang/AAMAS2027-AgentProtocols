\section{Intervention}
\label{sec:method}

We vary organization in both directions from one baseline: we remove it,
and we enforce it. All conditions share three agents, the same interaction order
(Planner~$\rightarrow$~Executor~$\rightarrow$~Reviewer), message-passing
graph, context visibility (except under enforcement, below), total call
budget, verifier and verifier prompt, retry budget, decoding parameters
(temperature $0$, identical \texttt{max\_tokens}), stopping rule (a PASS ends
the trajectory) and grading. Every trajectory in the frozen snapshot carries
the identical budget record: the design tests organization, not scale.
\textbf{Besides the roles, three things differ.} After a FAIL the evaluator returns a repair hint, written
with the gold answer in view and instructed not to reveal it; the
role-structured and enforced templates show it to all three agents in the
next cycle, each through its role's advice slot. The role-free template has
no such slot, so the role-free condition is reported both without and with
the feedback. The role-free footer asks every agent for an answer, and the
role-structured Reviewer carries two clauses the role-free prompt lacks (below).

\paragraph{Role-structured \PER{} (baseline).} Role prompts as ordinarily
written: the organization as specified, including a Reviewer told to
approve only a complete answer of the right shape and, in a cycle that
receives a FAIL, to route the work back to the Planner or Executor rather
than approve.

\paragraph{Role-free, feedback withheld.} Identical topology and
budget; all three agents get one byte-identical prompt: ``You are one of
three agents collaboratively solving this problem. Continue the work,''
the problem, the shared history, the agent's slot number, and a footer
asking for its current best answer in \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} and
a closing \texttt{[Agree]} (ready to submit) or \texttt{[Continue]} ---
the conventions the role-structured Executor and Reviewer use. Removed is
every clause saying who does what, above all the Planner's \textsc{must
not}s on computing and answering. In every prose condition the third
agent's \texttt{[Agree]} is approval (untagged counts as
\texttt{[Continue]}) and the latest boxed answer is submitted. Nothing in
this template carries the evaluator's verdict or hint.

\paragraph{Role-free with feedback.} The same role-free prompt, byte for
byte, plus the evaluator's verdict and repair hint --- the string the
role-structured team receives --- shown to all three agents as an
\texttt{[Evaluator]} entry after the shared history in the cycle after a
FAIL, the lifetime the advice slot has. It differs from the feedback-withheld
one only in feedback, and from the role-structured one in roles, in the
Reviewer's clauses and in feedback framing.

\paragraph{Structurally enforced.} Specialization is enforced by the
interface rather than by wording. The Planner emits JSON against
a schema with \emph{no answer field}, so a final answer is
unrepresentable; the Executor receives the parsed plan object and
the problem --- never the Planner's raw text --- and emits
\texttt{\{derivation, answer\}}; the Reviewer emits
\texttt{\{verdict, target\_span, critique\}}, and \texttt{accept}
promotes the Executor's answer verbatim \emph{by reference}, having no
field in which to \emph{author} one. Each function stays with one agent
under the same budget, but the Executor sees less context and a rejected
emission can exhaust the regeneration budget. A \emph{context-restored
control} repeats it with shared history restored.

\paragraph{Single-agent reference.} A budget-matched single-agent protocol
on the same problems, from a previously released corpus rather than this
campaign (\S\ref{sec:experiments}), guards against ``the system was one
agent all along''~\cite{zhang2026rethinking}. It cuts both ways: gemma-4's
role-structured and feedback-withheld role-free teams finish below their
single agent ($-0.0225$, $-0.0563$) and gpt-oss's feedback-withheld
role-free team above it ($+0.0294$); the arms come from separate runs
(supplement).

\subsection{Instrumentation and pre-registered analyses}
\label{subsec:prereg}

An intervention that never engaged cannot explain an outcome, so
engagement is measured rather than assumed: every
rejected-and-regenerated emission is logged with its reason, the
enforcement rate counts role-boundary violations only, and
answer-bearing calls are counted per condition. One instrumentation gap
escaped this discipline (\S\ref{subsec:logging}).

All five endpoints were fixed in writing and committed before any
intervention data existed; the full table, with what happened to each, is
in the supplement. Units are (problem, actor) pairs and all conditions run
the same problems, so every test is paired with bootstrap clustering by
problem. \textbf{The pre-registered primary endpoint is null}: no pairwise
difference-in-differences of P4 --- whether enforcement's effect on accuracy
differs by actor --- survives Holm (smallest adjusted $p=0.17$;
before correction the Llama-3.3$-$gemma-4 interval, $+0.040$ $[+0.001,+0.080]$,
only just excludes zero, and a separate resample gives $p=0.055$), and only the gpt-oss$-$gemma-4 contrast was
powered for an interaction of plausible size. The \Pone{} manipulation
objective --- enforcement driving Planner computation-abstention to
${\approx}1.0$ --- also failed: the schema forbids an \emph{answer}, not a
computation. It was re-designated to answer authorship. \textbf{The
role-structured-versus-role-free contrast this paper is organized around
was pre-registered as a secondary, exploratory replication of a published
null}; splitting it into phases is post hoc, and the role-free condition
with feedback was added after the snapshot was frozen, with its design and
primary analysis committed in writing before any of its data existed. Its results
are exploratory. Exclusions were
fixed in advance, including the rule that decides \S\ref{subsec:enforce}:
\emph{unparseable trajectories count as incorrect rather than dropped}.
