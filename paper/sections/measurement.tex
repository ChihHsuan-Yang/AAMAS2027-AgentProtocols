\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:measurement}

This section instantiates the two constructs of \S\ref{sec:framework} as
measurements, validates them against hand adjudication, and reports what they
show on matched Planner--Executor--Reviewer (\PER{}) trajectories before any
intervention. The corpus comprises 145{,}411 role messages. It is an
instrument here, not a contribution; a companion resource describes it as a
dataset.

\subsection{Role distinguishability}

We train a classifier to recover the emitting role from message behavior under
5-fold cross-validation grouped by trajectory, so that no trajectory is split
across folds. Two feature sets are used. The first includes prompt-template
markers -- artifacts of the scaffold rather than of behavior. The second, which
is the one we report, removes them, leaving only \emph{function-only} features.

Roles are highly distinguishable. Three-way balanced accuracy is $0.9416$ with
all features and $\mathbf{0.8964}$ function-only, against a chance rate of
$0.333$; Planner versus Executor reaches $\mathrm{AUC}=0.9985$ and $0.9954$
respectively, against $0.500$. \textbf{The strong hypothesis that role prompts
fail to induce distinct behavior is false and we abandon it.}

What the roles are distinguishable \emph{by} is the finding
(Table~\ref{tab:singlefeature}). Permutation importance ranks numbered steps
($0.068$), boxed commitment ($0.050$), and character count ($0.047$) far above
evaluated-equation density ($0.013$). Restricting the classifier to computation
features alone drops Planner-versus-Executor AUC from $0.995$ to $\mathbf{0.714}$
-- \emph{below} a length-only nuisance control at $0.768$. The roles are
separable by how they format output and whether they commit an answer, not by
the division of cognitive labor the contract specifies. This is the dissociation
that motivates measuring fidelity separately.

\subsection{Contract-derived fidelity measures}

Fidelity is scored clause-by-clause against the contract the agent actually
received. Contracts differ by domain in this corpus: the full contract is
present only in the competition-mathematics domain, while other domain presets
impose only answer-abstention, plan-reference, and routing clauses. We
therefore score each clause only where its own contract imposes it, report
components separately, and \textbf{never average fidelity across domains or
across clauses}. A single fidelity scalar would be the main way to get this
wrong, and \S\ref{subsec:components-disagree} shows it would hide a real
disagreement.

Two clause measures are frozen and used throughout:

\begin{description}
\item[\Pone{} -- Planner abstains from computing.] A Planner message scores $1$
  if it contains no evaluated numeric equality. The contract explicitly permits
  restating givens and naming formulas symbolically, so those do not count as
  violations; producing a value not in the problem statement does. Scored on
  competition mathematics only -- the sole domain whose prompt carries the
  clause.
\item[\Etwo{} -- Executor consumes the plan.] An Executor message scores $1$ if
  it explicitly references the plan or the Planner. Bare step numbering is
  excluded, because the Executor numbers its own working.
\end{description}

Annotation definitions, including edge rules, were written and frozen
\emph{before} the adjudication sheet was opened, so that they could not be
tuned to flatter the automated measures.

reason for every label (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). Both measures clear a
pre-set agreement threshold of $0.80$.

Two properties of the error structure matter more than the headline figures.
First, \Pone{} agreement \emph{fell} from $0.967$ at $n=30$ to $0.903$ at
$n=72$ as adjudication continued. The early figure was optimistic; we report
the final one, and we note the direction because it is the normal one and the
reason to finish a validation sheet rather than stop at a good number. Second,
the residual errors fall into two classes pointing in \emph{opposite}
directions. The two false positives are symbolic results carrying no digits --
a Planner asserting a ratio of $\pi/(4\sqrt{3})$ while stating that it performs
no arithmetic -- which the digit-based pattern cannot see; this means \Pone{}
slightly \emph{over-credits} compliance, so true violation rates are marginally
higher than we report. The three false negatives are permitted content:
restated givens, and search instructions addressed to the Executor. Because the
two classes push in opposite directions, they do not bias the actor comparison
in a consistent way.

One systematic error was found and repaired rather than tolerated. The first
version of the Executor measure matched phrases such as ``Step 0'' and
``Inductive step'' inside the Executor's \emph{own} construction -- precisely
the solving-versus-following confusion the construct is meant to avoid. The
measure was rebuilt to require an explicit plan or Planner reference, and only
that version (\Etwo{}) is used.

\subsection{A rejected ``improvement''}

We also built a \Pone{} variant (v3) adding an instruction/given exclusion and a
symbolic-result detector, intended to close the false-positive class above.
\textbf{It was rejected: agreement fell from $0.903$ to $0.694$ and recall from
$0.868$ to $0.421$.} The symbolic detector fired on $9$ of $9$ messages judged
compliant, including on the phrase ``The answer should be a single integer'' --
which is the contract's \emph{required} answer-form section. The ``improved''
metric penalized compliance with the contract it was scoring. We report this
because the variant is the obvious next thing a reader would propose, and
because the documented limitation is preferable to a fix that is worse: \Pone{}
is frozen at v1 with its blind spot stated, not silently upgraded.

\subsection{Fidelity is uneven across clauses of one contract}

Within a single Planner contract, compliance ranges from $0.961$ (decomposes
the problem) and $0.936$ (declares the answer form) down to $0.042$ (abstains
from computing). Two \textsc{must not} clauses in the same prompt block diverge
sharply: abstains-from-answering is honored at $0.540$ while
abstains-from-computing is honored at $0.042$. The median Planner message under
that actor carries eight evaluated equations. Executor plan consumption is
weaker still under the same actor at $0.062$ (\Etwo{}, Table~\ref{tab:actors}):
the plan is injected directly into the Executor's prompt, and it is explicitly
referenced in roughly one message in sixteen. Contract compliance is therefore
not a property of a contract but of each clause within it, which is the second
reason we refuse to average.

\subsection{Realized fidelity is actor-dependent, and its components disagree}
\label{subsec:components-disagree}

Within the competition-mathematics domain every actor receives a
\emph{byte-identical} Planner prompt. Realized fidelity nonetheless differs
sharply across actors (Table~\ref{tab:actors}), and all six pairwise
differences exclude zero.

The same written organization, executed by different substrates, is realized
differently -- and the difference is not small: the largest \Pone{} gap is
$+0.225$ $[+0.210,+0.239]$.

\textbf{The two components order the actors differently.} On \Pone{}, actor A
leads; on \Etwo{}, actor B leads. Only the 120B actor is last on both. There is
no single most-faithful actor, and we do not construct one. Reporting a scalar
average would have manufactured a ranking that neither component supports, and
any mediation claim must therefore name its component in advance. We report the
disagreement rather than reconcile it.

\subsection{Does fidelity predict success? A leakage correction}

An apparent association between trajectory-mean fidelity and correctness
($+0.61$) is an artifact and we discard it. Message count correlates $-0.910$
with first-pass correctness, because the protocol halts on agreement and
failing trajectories accumulate additional low-fidelity retry rounds --
outcome-dependent stopping contaminating any trajectory-level average.

Restricting to the \emph{first} Planner message, emitted before the Executor
runs and before any verdict exists, and to the single \Pone{} clause, compliant
Planner messages are followed by first-pass success $+0.127$ more often than
violating ones, with the same point estimate in two independent actors
($[+0.077,+0.179]$ and $[+0.071,+0.180]$). Stratifying by difficulty deflates
this further: the effect is $+0.171$ $[+0.096,+0.244]$ and $+0.0845$
$[+0.0565,+0.1084]$ on problems the one-shot baseline solved, but $+0.0245$
$[-0.0175,+0.0664]$ and $+0.0676$ $[-0.0026,+0.1426]$ on problems it did not --
null in both actors. Compliance is itself difficulty-linked, so residual
confounding remains even within strata. The honest statement is that Planner
compliance is associated with success on tractable problems, is not detectably
associated on hard ones, and is observationally confounded. Nothing here
licenses a causal claim, which is why \S\ref{sec:method} intervenes.
