\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All statistical contrasts are paired at the problem level, with bootstrap
intervals clustered by problem. Trajectories that never produce a graded
answer count as incorrect, except in the first-pass contrast of
\S\ref{subsec:collapse}. Significance is the exact McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} on discordant pairs (problems that exactly one
condition gets right), with $p$-values to two significant figures.

\subsection{R1. Removing roles changes the organization, not the first
answer}
\label{subsec:collapse}

We delete the role clauses, and the role-free prompt also asks every agent
for its current best answer. The agents, interaction order, budget and
evaluator are unchanged. We measure whether \emph{every} agent asserts its
own final answer in the first cycle, where trajectory length cannot confound
the metric (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). This rate rises from between $0.000$
and $0.120$ to between $0.846$ and $0.976$. Decomposed by role, the Planner
supplies most of the change. The Executor answers in both conditions, and
the Reviewer's rate, already high for two actors, rises in all three, but
the Planner's moves from $0.000$, $0.183$ and $0.000$ to $0.979$, $0.898$
and $0.889$ (the gemma-4, gpt-oss and Llama-3.3 models). Since the footer
asks for an answer, this measures what a Planner does once its \textsc{must
not} clause is removed, not an emergent tendency; the claim concerns this
\emph{change}, not the absolute level. Under three answer-marker definitions
the role-free rate stays between $0.846$ and $0.999$ while the
role-structured rate reaches $0.892$ only under the loosest marker, and the
ordering holds in all nine actor by marker cells (supplement).

\paragraph{Content duplication and a second opinion.} For gemma-4 and
Llama-3.3, the role-free Planners write full independent solutions before
boxing an answer, and the overlap between the numbers in their messages and
those in the Executor's exceeds the overlap between Planner and Executor
messages from different problems in the same condition by $+0.449$ and
$+0.432$ ($+0.159$ and $+0.145$ with roles). A strong commercial model
judging $171$ trajectories against a frozen rubric agrees with the
functional-collapse measure (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}; repeat agreement
$0.953$). This adjudication is outcome-blind (the judge did not see the
grade) but not condition-blind: role words were renamed rather than
equalized, and a renamed token appears in all $42$ role-structured items but
only $3$ of $56$ role-free ones (Fisher's exact test,
$p=1.5\times10^{-24}$). The collapse claim does not rest on it.

\paragraph{First-pass accuracy does not follow.} On problems graded under
both conditions ($n=1{,}600$ / $1{,}592$ / $389$), first-pass accuracy is
\emph{identical} for gemma-4 ($+0.0000$, $[-0.0156,+0.0150]$) and flat for
Llama-3.3 ($+0.0077$), while it \emph{improves} for gpt-oss ($+0.0503$,
$[+0.0339,+0.0666]$; Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}A).

\paragraph{A logging defect.} \label{subsec:logging} Our runner logged each
response's \texttt{content} field, falling back to its \texttt{reasoning}
field, the hidden reasoning trace, only when the content was empty.
gpt-oss-120b often returns a short content string beside a long hidden
trace, so its derivations are missing from the record: $75.7\%$ of its
role-free messages and $93.4\%$ of its first cycles (the flag meant to
report this reports $5.8\%$). Agents pass content only, by design and in
every condition, so the defect is in the record alone, and no point estimate
of gpt-oss's duplication is recoverable. It does not manufacture the
collapse: in first cycles with no affected message, role-free collapse is
$0.821$ ($n=106$) and role-structured $0.118$.

\subsection{R2. Given the same feedback, the role-free team recovers more}
\label{subsec:recovery}

\paragraph{What a cycle is.} The pipeline runs a fixed grid of six
deliberation cycles, three outer rounds of two inner rounds, each one pass
of Planner, Executor and Reviewer; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows how a
cycle ends in a \emph{submission} or a \emph{revision}.

\input{tables/main_results}

\paragraph{Recovery and final accuracy.} The role-structured team receives
the evaluator's feedback, so the like-for-like comparison is with the
role-free team that also receives it (Table~\ref{tab:main}). That team
recovers more: $0.609$ against $0.222$ (marginal, that is, each team over
its own failed trajectories; Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}B). On the $236$
problems both teams first answer wrongly, $89$ are recovered only without
roles and $4$ only with them ($p=6.2\times10^{-22}$). Both reach the same
first-pass rate, and a correct first answer is never lost, so this carries
into final accuracy: $0.921$ against $0.846$ ($p=1.7\times10^{-24}$,
conditioned on nothing). The recoveries are revisions, not re-gradings: in
$192$ of the $193$, the passing answer differs from the one first rejected
($541$ of $547$ across the five feedback-matched cells), and repeated
identical evaluator prompts disagree in only $0.0072$ of cases.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and leakage.} A second, independent run of the
two gemma-4 feedback-matched cells is close to the first, though the paired
test on the SciBench benchmark weakens from $p=0.013$ to $0.052$; we report
the first run throughout (supplement). Repair hints contain the gold answer
verbatim in at most $2.3\%$ of failing verdicts in any cell, and dropping
every gemma-4 trajectory on the Omni-MATH benchmark that saw one leaves
recovery at $0.599$ and $0.212$. We did not audit partial leakage.

\paragraph{Scope across actors.} Both other actors repeat the ordering in
recovery and final accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and in both the feedback
leaves first-pass accuracy where it was ($0.765$ and $0.317$, against
$0.762$ and $0.315$ with it withheld). Llama-3.3's role-structured team
rarely submits; there, enforcement recovers about as much as the role-free
team with feedback ($0.139$ against $0.147$ marginally; paired $9$ against
$17$ of $219$, $p=0.17$). Against the feedback-withheld baseline, the
role-structured advantage is clear only for gemma-4. It is directional for
gpt-oss ($0.324$ against $0.247$; paired $52$ against $39$ of $327$,
$p=0.21$) and absent for Llama-3.3 ($0.014$ against $0.015$). gpt-oss
recovers without feedback, consistent with its answer moving from cycle to
cycle anyway: one distinct answer in $20.8\%$ of its failed trajectories,
against $84.5\%$ for gemma-4.

\subsection{R3. Matched on verdicts, the gap closes; roles gate
submission}
\label{subsec:mechanism}

\paragraph{The feedback-withheld baseline.} Without the feedback, the
role-free team turns nearly its whole budget into submissions. Across
gemma-4's failed trajectories, it submits in $5.80$ of $5.83$ cycles
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C), its third agent never declines in $98.1\%$
of these trajectories, and in $84.5\%$ the transcript holds exactly one
distinct answer. Nothing in its prompts carries the feedback, so each FAIL
is followed by another cycle over the same history. Given the verdict and
hint, the identical prompts produce a different team. The answer moves (one
distinct answer in $6.6\%$ of failed trajectories), and recovery rises from
$0.051$ to $0.609$. Of the $265$ problems both first answer wrongly, $139$
are recovered only with the feedback and $2$ only without it
($p=7.2\times10^{-39}$). First-pass accuracy is $0.80$ in both conditions,
and $7$ of the $16$ recoveries without feedback are the same answer graded
again. For gemma-4, revision requires the feedback rather than the roles.

\paragraph{Matched on verdicts, the gap closes.} The role-free team with
feedback also receives more of it: each submission returns a verdict and, on
a FAIL, a hint written with the gold answer in view. To compare the teams at
equal exposure, a post-hoc analysis takes the problems both first answer
wrongly and cuts each role-free trajectory off after as many evaluator
verdicts as the role-structured team received on the same problem. No cell
then shows a significant difference (Table~\ref{tab:main}; $p\geq0.48$), and
Llama-3.3's role-structured team submits so rarely that its cell has five
events. Pooled and stratified by cell, the difference is $+0.009$ in favor
of roles ($95\%$ paired bootstrap confidence interval $-0.014$ to $+0.033$).
The match is coarse: where the role-structured team received one verdict,
the pair ties at zero by construction ($141$ of gemma-4's $236$ pairs).
Restricted to problems where it received at least two, the difference is
$+0.018$ ($-0.029$ to $+0.065$); recoding the scoring defect of
\S\ref{sec:limitations} gives $+0.029$ ($+0.004$ to $+0.054$; supplement).
Because the comparison crosses the freeze, we repeat it against the
post-freeze role-structured re-run. On the $65$ problems both first answer
wrongly, the role-free team leads $22$ to $3$ unmatched
($p=1.6\times10^{-4}$), but roles lead $4$ to $1$ verdict-matched
($p=0.38$). No variant puts the role-free team significantly ahead verdict
for verdict; the only significant variant favors roles, by a small margin.
The role-free team recovers more because it asks for more verdicts.

\paragraph{What roles do instead: a gate on submission.} The gap in verdicts
comes from the role-structured Reviewer. Across gemma-4's failed
trajectories, that team submits $1.27$ times in $5.52$ cycles, against
$3.20$ in $3.96$ without roles (Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C). Of its
declining cycles, $53\%$ come before the first submission, when no feedback
exists, and $84$ of its $316$ failed trajectories never submit within the
budget; they are graded once at the end and never see the feedback. The
clause forbidding approval in a cycle that receives a FAIL covers only
$19\%$ of the declines. In later cycles that receive no FAIL, where approval
is allowed, the Reviewer still declines in $75\%$ of $504$ such cycles,
against $4\%$ of $184$ for the role-free team's third agent. On Omni-MATH,
the clause covers $37\%$ of gpt-oss's declines and $4\%$ of Llama-3.3's. On
SciBench, it covers $40\%$ of gemma-4's declines and $60\%$ of gpt-oss's
(supplement). The gate might look like precision, since recoveries per
verdict after the first are higher with roles ($0.41$ against $0.28$ for
gemma-4), but that compares different trajectories; on the same problem and
the same number of verdicts, the two teams recover about equally.

\paragraph{Removing the gate alone.} A direct, exploratory test keeps the
role-structured prompts byte for byte but submits the team's answer in every
cycle, whatever the Reviewer decides. We ran it once, for gemma-4 on the
$400$ Omni-MATH problems of the post-freeze re-run (supplement). First-pass
accuracy is unchanged ($0.768$ against $0.773$), the team draws $4.20$
verdicts per failed trajectory against $1.44$ for the gated re-run, and
recovery rises from $0.198$ to $0.613$, as high as the role-free team's
$0.549$ on the same problems. On problems both first answer wrongly, the
forced-submission team recovers $26$ that the gated one misses and $2$ the
other way ($p=3.0\times10^{-6}$); against the role-free team, the split is
$7$ to $7$ ($p=1$). With the role contract intact, removing the gate removed
the gap.

\subsection{R4. The same ordering in a second domain}
\label{subsec:xdomain}

SciBench's complete text-only slice of $574$ free-response college science
problems, frozen separately, tests whether the pattern belongs to the
organization or to competition mathematics. The role-free prompt is
byte-identical to its Omni-MATH form; the role-structured prompt keeps the
same structural contract with domain wording adapted, as does the evaluator
prompt. For both actors the ordering returns (Table~\ref{tab:main}):
first-pass accuracy is flat, recovery is highest with feedback and no roles,
and final accuracy is higher without roles. The recovery contrast is weaker
and, as on Omni-MATH, vanishes when both teams are credited with the same
number of verdicts. One behavior does not transfer: gemma-4's
role-structured answer does not lock, that is, stay the same across cycles
(one distinct answer in $7.6\%$ of failed trajectories, against $8.5\%$ for
the role-free team with feedback), and by the second verdict the two recover
alike ($0.237$ against $0.239$; supplement).

\subsection{R5. Structural enforcement shifts the allocation, and
introduces brittleness}
\label{subsec:enforce}

Enforced and role-structured prompts carry the same advice slot, so this
contrast is matched on feedback access. Enforcement replaces the behavioral
request with a constraint: the Planner's final answer becomes
unrepresentable. The manipulation check passes. The Planner asserts a final
answer in $0.000$ of enforced first cycles in all three actors, against
$0.979$, $0.898$ and $0.889$ without roles, and enforcement logs $838$,
$1{,}183$ and $395$ role-boundary rejections per cell. It does not change
what the Planner \emph{computes}, so the adjudication still finds no
functional division in $0.071$ to $0.188$ of enforced first cycles: an agent
can be structurally stopped from answering without being stopped from doing
the work. Relative to the role-structured team, enforcement loosens the
gate. The enforced team submits more often ($2.02$, $2.33$ and $1.84$ per
failed trajectory against $1.27$, $2.10$ and $0.32$ for gemma-4, gpt-oss and
Llama-3.3) and, for gemma-4 and Llama-3.3, unlocks the answer: one distinct
answer in $0.119$ and $0.072$ of failed trajectories against $0.288$ and
$0.750$ with roles (for gpt-oss, $0.092$ against $0.052$).

Recovery follows this loosening most clearly for the weakest actor:
Llama-3.3's rises about tenfold, from $0.014$ to $0.139$
($p=3.8\times10^{-6}$). For gemma-4 ($0.314$ against $0.222$, $p=0.30$) and
gpt-oss ($0.395$ against $0.324$, $p=0.11$), the direction is the same but
not resolvable against attrition (trajectories that never finish). Recovery
counts every trajectory with a recorded incorrect first verdict, including
the $150$ enforced trajectories that then died in regeneration, scored $0$
by the pre-registration.

Improved revisability does not become end-to-end accuracy, because
enforcement is brittle. Under the pre-registered rule, the accuracy
difference is $-0.0225$, $-0.0050$ and $+0.0175$ for gemma-4, gpt-oss and
Llama-3.3: one significant negative and two nulls. Of enforced trajectories,
$8.0\%$, $9.9\%$ and $17.0\%$ never finish, against $0.0$ to $0.8\%$
role-structured and $0.0$ to $2.5\%$ role-free. Dropping them gives
$+0.0190$, $+0.0361$ and $+0.0453$: all positive, all significant, ordered
by actor weakness, matching the direction reported for the capability
equalizer~\cite{canedo2026architecture}. That second reading is survivorship
bias: it scores only the trajectories that finished, whereas the
pre-registration fixed in advance that the others count as incorrect.
Restoring shared history at fixed enforcement raises gemma-4's
pre-registered accuracy ($+0.0450$, $p=0.0079$; as graded $+0.0196$,
$p=0.14$) but not gpt-oss's ($-0.0125$, $p=0.56$; both scorings with
intervals in the supplement).
