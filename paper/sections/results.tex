\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All statistical contrasts are paired at the problem level, with bootstrap
intervals clustered by problem. Trajectories that never produce a graded
answer count as incorrect, except in the first-pass contrast of
\S\ref{subsec:collapse}. Significance is the exact McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} on discordant pairs (problems that exactly one
condition gets right).

\subsection{R1. Removing roles changes the organization, not the first
answer}
\label{subsec:collapse}

We delete the role clauses and keep the agents, interaction order, budget
and evaluator; the role-free footer also asks every agent for its current
best answer. Functional collapse, the share of first cycles in which every
agent asserts its own final answer, rises from at most $0.120$ with roles to
at least $0.846$ without them for all three actors, the gemma-4, gpt-oss and
Llama-3.3 models (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). Most of the change comes
from the Planner (per-role rates in the supplement). The Executor answers in
both conditions, and for two actors the Reviewer states an answer even with
roles, plausibly because approving an answer tends to restate it. The
Planner seldom answers with roles (at most $0.183$) and nearly always does
without them, so the division of labor rests on one prohibition, its
\textsc{must not} clause. Because the role-free footer asks for an answer,
the absolute level partly reflects that request; the claim concerns the
\emph{change}. The ordering holds for every actor under all three
answer-marker definitions (the cues that count as asserting an answer;
supplement).

\paragraph{Content duplication and a second opinion.} For the gemma-4 and
Llama-3.3 models, the role-free Planners write full independent solutions before boxing an
answer, and their messages share far more numbers with the Executor's than
messages from different problems do, and far more than with roles
(supplement). The Executor thus redoes the Planner's computation instead
of carrying out a plan. A
frontier model judging a sample of first cycles against a frozen rubric
agrees with the functional-collapse measure (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}), and
its repeated judgments almost always agree (supplement). This adjudication
is outcome-blind (the judge did not see the grade) but not condition-blind:
role words were renamed rather than equalized across conditions, so a
renamed token appears in all $42$ role-structured items but in only $3$ of
$56$ role-free ones. The collapse claim does not rest on it.

\paragraph{First-pass accuracy does not follow.} The collapse costs no
accuracy: on problems graded both with roles and without them (feedback
withheld), first-pass accuracy is \emph{identical} for gemma-4 ($+0.0000$,
$[-0.0156,+0.0150]$), flat for Llama-3.3 and higher without roles for
gpt-oss ($+0.0503$, $[+0.0339,+0.0666]$; Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}A).
Because one model plays every agent, a Planner that solves the problem
brings no competence the Executor lacks. The gpt-oss model may gain because its
answers vary between derivations (\S\ref{subsec:recovery}), so extra
independent solutions add chances to be right; we did not test this.

\paragraph{A logging defect.} \label{subsec:logging} Our runner logged
each response's \texttt{content} field and fell back to its
\texttt{reasoning} field (the hidden reasoning trace) only when the content
was empty. The gpt-oss-120b model often returns a short content string
beside a long hidden trace, so most of its role-free derivations ($75.7\%$
of messages) are missing from the record, and the flag meant to report this
misses most of them (supplement). Agents pass content only, by design and
in every condition, so the defect is in the record alone, but no point
estimate of gpt-oss's duplication is recoverable. It does not manufacture
the collapse: in first cycles with no affected message ($106$ without
roles), role-free collapse is $0.821$ and role-structured $0.118$.

\subsection{R2. Given the same feedback, the role-free team recovers more}
\label{subsec:recovery}

\paragraph{What a cycle is.} The pipeline runs a fixed grid of six
deliberation cycles, three outer rounds of two inner rounds, each one pass
of Planner, Executor and Reviewer; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows how a
cycle ends in a \emph{submission} or a \emph{revision}.

\input{tables/main_results}

\paragraph{Recovery and final accuracy.} The role-structured team receives
the evaluator's feedback, so the like-for-like comparison is with the
role-free team that also receives it (Table~\ref{tab:main}). That team
recovers more in all five cells. For gemma-4 on Omni-MATH it recovers
$0.609$ of its failed trajectories against $0.222$ (each team over its own;
Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}B), and of the $236$ problems both first
answer wrongly, $89$ are recovered only without roles and $4$ only with
them ($p=6.2\times10^{-22}$). Both teams reach the same first-pass rate and
never lose a correct first answer, so the extra recoveries carry straight
into final accuracy ($0.921$ against $0.846$). They are genuine revisions:
nearly every passing answer differs from the one first rejected, and
identical evaluator prompts almost never disagree (supplement).

\paragraph{Leakage and reproducibility.} Because the evaluator writes hints
with the gold answer in view, a hint could hand over the answer. Hints
contain it verbatim in at most $2.3\%$ of failing verdicts in any cell, and
dropping every gemma-4 trajectory on the Omni-MATH benchmark that saw one
barely moves either recovery rate (supplement); we did not audit partial
leakage. A second, independent run of the two gemma-4 feedback-matched
cells is close to the first, though the paired test on the SciBench
benchmark weakens from $p=0.013$ to $0.052$; we report the first run
throughout (supplement).

\paragraph{Why the role effect differs by actor.} The other two actors
repeat the ordering (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and the feedback leaves their
first-pass accuracy unchanged, as it must: the two role-free conditions are
identical until the first verdict. Against the feedback-withheld baseline,
the role-structured advantage depends on how much an actor needs feedback
to change its answer. It is clear for gemma-4, whose answer rarely moves
without feedback (one distinct answer in $84.5\%$ of failed trajectories,
against $20.8\%$ for gpt-oss), directional for gpt-oss ($0.324$ against
$0.247$; $p=0.21$), which revises even without feedback, and absent for
Llama-3.3 ($0.014$ against $0.015$), whose role-structured team almost
never submits and so rarely sees the feedback its prompts could carry.

\subsection{R3. Matched on verdicts, the gap closes; roles gate
submission}
\label{subsec:mechanism}

\paragraph{The feedback-withheld baseline.} Without the feedback, the
role-free team cannot revise. It submits in almost every cycle
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C), but nothing in its prompts tells the
agents that the answer failed, so the next cycle repeats it
(\S\ref{subsec:recovery}); for gemma-4, $7$ of its $16$ recoveries are the
same answer graded again. Given the verdict and hint, the identical prompts
produce a team that revises. The answer moves (a single distinct answer in
only $6.6\%$ of failed trajectories), and recovery rises from $0.051$ to
$0.609$; of the $265$ problems both first answer wrongly, $139$ are
recovered only with the feedback and $2$ only without it
($p=7.2\times10^{-39}$). For gemma-4, revision requires the feedback rather
than the roles.

\paragraph{Matched on verdicts, the gap closes.} The role-free team with
feedback also receives more of it. Each submission returns a verdict and,
on a FAIL, a hint written with the gold answer in view, so every submission
is another chance to revise. To compare the teams at equal exposure, a
post-hoc analysis takes the problems both first answer wrongly and cuts
each role-free trajectory off after as many evaluator verdicts as the
role-structured team received on the same problem. No cell then shows a
significant difference (Table~\ref{tab:main}; $p\geq0.48$; Llama-3.3's cell
has five events). Pooled and stratified by cell, the difference is $+0.009$
in favor of roles ($95\%$ paired bootstrap interval $-0.014$ to $+0.033$).
The match is coarse: a role-free trajectory cut at a single verdict, a
FAIL, cannot recover, so wherever the role-structured team received one
verdict the pair ties at zero by construction, as in most of gemma-4's
pairs. Restricted to problems with at least two such verdicts, the
difference is $+0.018$ ($-0.029$ to $+0.065$); recoding the scoring defect
of \S\ref{sec:limitations} gives $+0.029$ ($+0.004$ to $+0.054$), and
repeating the comparison against the post-freeze role-structured re-run
gives the same pattern (supplement). No variant puts the role-free team
significantly ahead verdict for verdict; the only significant variant
favors roles, by a small margin. Given the same feedback, the two teams use
it about equally well; the role-free team recovers more because it asks for
more.

\paragraph{What roles do instead: a gate on submission.} The gap in
verdicts comes from the role-structured Reviewer. For gemma-4 that team
submits $1.27$ times per failed trajectory, against $3.20$ without roles
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C). Much of the withholding happens before any
feedback exists: $53\%$ of the Reviewer's declines come before the first
submission, and $84$ of the $316$ failed trajectories never submit, so they
are graded once at the end and never see the feedback. The clause that
forbids approval right after a FAIL covers only $19\%$ of the declines; in
later cycles where approval is allowed, the Reviewer still declines in
$75\%$ of cycles, against $4\%$ for the role-free team's third agent. On
Omni-MATH the clause covers at most $37\%$ of the declines for any actor,
so there the gate reflects the Reviewer's disposition rather than the
letter of its prompt, plausibly because an agent told to critique finds
something to criticize; on SciBench the clause covers more (supplement).
Higher recoveries per later verdict with roles ($0.41$ against $0.28$ for
gemma-4) can look like precision, but that ratio compares different
trajectories (supplement).

\paragraph{Removing the gate alone.} If the gate causes the gap, removing
it with the role prompts untouched should close the gap. A direct,
exploratory test keeps the role-structured prompts byte for byte but
submits the team's answer in every cycle, whatever the Reviewer decides. We
ran it once, for gemma-4 on the $400$ Omni-MATH problems of the post-freeze
re-run (supplement). The team then draws far more verdicts ($4.20$ against
$1.44$ per failed trajectory), and recovery rises from $0.198$ to $0.613$,
as high as the role-free team's $0.549$ on the same problems (paired, $26$
against $2$, $p=3.0\times10^{-6}$; against the role-free team, $7$ against
$7$). First-pass accuracy does not move, so the Reviewer's declines before
any feedback were not buying a better first answer either. With the role
contract intact, removing the gate removed the gap.

\subsection{R4. The same ordering in a second domain}
\label{subsec:xdomain}

The SciBench benchmark's complete text-only slice of $574$ free-response
college science problems, frozen separately, tests whether the pattern
belongs to the organization or to competition mathematics. The role-free
prompt is byte-identical across domains; the role-structured and evaluator
prompts adapt only their domain wording (supplement). For both actors run
there, the gemma-4 and gpt-oss models, the ordering returns
(Table~\ref{tab:main}): first-pass accuracy is flat, recovery is highest
with feedback and no roles, and final accuracy is higher without roles. The
recovery contrast is weaker, consistent with a looser gate: the
role-structured team almost always submits at least once (all but $3$ and
$1$ of its failed trajectories), so more of the feedback reaches it, and,
as on Omni-MATH, the contrast vanishes when both teams are credited with
the same number of verdicts. Unlike on Omni-MATH, gemma-4's
role-structured answer does not lock here, possibly because of the looser
gate (supplement).

\subsection{R5. Structural enforcement shifts the allocation, and
introduces brittleness}
\label{subsec:enforce}

Enforced and role-structured prompts carry the same advice slot, so this
contrast is matched on feedback access. Enforcement replaces the behavioral
request with a constraint: the Planner's final answer becomes
unrepresentable. The manipulation check passes: the Planner asserts a final
answer in $0.000$ of enforced first cycles for all three actors, against at
least $0.889$ without roles. Enforcement does not change what the Planner
\emph{computes}, so the adjudication still finds no functional division in
$0.071$ to $0.188$ of enforced first cycles: an agent can be structurally
stopped from answering without being stopped from doing the work. It does
loosen the gate: the enforced team submits more often for every actor and,
with more hints arriving, unlocks the answer for gemma-4 and Llama-3.3
(supplement).

Recovery rises most where the gate was tightest. Llama-3.3's
role-structured team almost never submits, and under enforcement its
recovery rises about tenfold, from $0.014$ to $0.139$
($p=3.8\times10^{-6}$), close to the role-free team's $0.147$. For gemma-4
and gpt-oss the direction is the same ($p=0.30$ and $0.11$) but not
resolvable against attrition: the $150$ enforced trajectories that died
after an incorrect first verdict count as unrecovered, as the
pre-registration requires.

Improved revisability does not become end-to-end accuracy, because
enforcement is brittle. A malformed or boundary-violating output must be
regenerated, and when the regeneration budget runs out the trajectory dies:
$8.0\%$, $9.9\%$ and $17.0\%$ of enforced trajectories never finish,
against at most $2.5\%$ in any other condition, and the weakest actor
breaks the schema most often (supplement). Under the pre-registered rule,
which counts these trajectories as incorrect, enforcement changes accuracy
by $-0.0225$, $-0.0050$ and $+0.0175$ for gemma-4, gpt-oss and Llama-3.3:
one significant negative and two nulls. Dropping them instead gives gains
for all three actors, all significant and ordered by actor weakness,
matching the direction reported for the capability
equalizer~\cite{canedo2026architecture}. That reading is survivorship bias,
since it scores only the trajectories that finished. For gemma-4, part of
the brittleness is lost context: restoring the shared history at fixed
enforcement raises its pre-registered accuracy ($+0.0450$, $p=0.0079$),
mostly through fewer trajectories dying, since as graded the change is not
significant; gpt-oss does not gain (supplement).
