\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All contrasts are paired at the problem level with bootstrap intervals
clustered by problem; trajectories never producing a graded answer count as
incorrect (the first-pass contrast of \S\ref{subsec:collapse} excepted),
and significance is exact McNemar on discordant pairs.

\subsection{R1. Removing roles changes the organization, not the first
answer}
\label{subsec:collapse}

Removing the role instructions changes nothing else: same three agents,
same order, same budget, same evaluator. The claim rests on mechanical
measures that involve no judge. We ask whether \emph{every}
agent independently asserts its own final answer on the first pass, so the
measure is not length-confounded (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}); once the
Planner's \textsc{must not} is gone and every agent is asked for its
current best answer, it rises from $0.000$--$0.120$ to $0.846$--$0.976$. \textbf{Decomposed by role, the
planner supplies almost all of the change}: the executor is at ceiling
either way and the reviewer was already high, while the planner moves
$0.000$/$0.183$/$0.000$ to $0.979$/$0.898$/$0.889$ (gemma-4 / gpt-oss /
Llama-3.3) --- a claim about the \emph{change}, not the level.

\input{tables/main_role_collapse}

\paragraph{Content duplication, against the right null.} gemma-4's and
Llama-3.3's role-free planners write full independent solutions
(${\approx}1{,}880$ characters, median) before boxing an answer.
Numeric-token overlap confirms it, but only against a
\emph{per-condition} null: pairing planners with executors from different
problems shows the baseline is itself condition-dependent, so the raw
difference is invalid. Against each condition's own null the excess shifts
$+0.290$ and $+0.287$ when roles are removed. gpt-oss cannot be
read at all: its role-free planner emits a median $37$ visible characters
--- the logging defect below.

\paragraph{A second opinion, and the leak that stops it being blind.} We
put 171 trajectories to a frontier model against a rubric frozen before
the first judgement, with actor, condition, correctness and the automated
label withheld and role words neutralized (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}, last
column; stability $0.953$). It is model adjudication, not human
annotation, and outcome-blind but \emph{not} condition-blind: neutralization renamed the role words without
equalizing their \emph{presence} --- a neutralized token appears in all $42$
role-structured items and $3$ of $56$ role-free ones (Fisher exact
$p=1.5\times10^{-24}$) --- so a judge noticing only whether a transcript
labels its speakers could separate the conditions without reading content.
Normalizing to a common surface would destroy the differences being judged,
so we disclose rather than re-run; we do not call it blinded, and the
collapse claim does not rest on it. Judge stability and the byte metric's precision/recall are
within-item, so unaffected.

\paragraph{First-pass accuracy does not follow.} The ablation removed the
organization without crippling the solver, the parity prior role ablations
report~\cite{dylan2026equal,orogat2026architectural}. On problems graded
under both conditions ($n=1{,}600$ / $1{,}592$ / $389$) the first-pass
difference is \emph{identical} for gemma-4 ($+0.0000$,
$[-0.0156,+0.0150]$) and flat for Llama-3.3 ($+0.0077$), while it
\emph{improves} for gpt-oss ($+0.0503$, $[+0.0339,+0.0666]$;
Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}A). Whatever roles buy, they do not buy the
first answer.

\paragraph{A logging defect, and its second consequence.}
\label{subsec:logging}
Our runner logged each response's \texttt{content}, falling back to
\texttt{reasoning} only when content was empty; gpt-oss-120b often returns
a short content string alongside a long hidden trace, so the derivation was
discarded while the flag documenting the substitution stayed false. The
same string is what the next agent received, identically in every
condition: the defect is in the record, not the run.
$75.7\%$ of gpt-oss role-free messages are affected and $93.4\%$
of its first passes contain one; the flag reports $5.8\%$, and the other
two actors are unaffected. Two consequences follow and no third.
No point estimate of gpt-oss's duplication rate is recoverable,
and the defect bounds collapse-\emph{mode} interpretation:
calling that 37-character planner a behavioral mode would attribute our
defect to the model. \textbf{It does not manufacture the collapse}, the
one place it could reach a headline measure: restricted to first passes
with \emph{no} affected message, role-free collapse is $0.821$ ($n=106$)
against $0.846$ overall and the role-structured rate $0.118$ against
$0.120$ --- the gap is intact on clean messages.

\subsection{R2. Roles change how a team responds to correctness feedback}
\label{subsec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_v1_story.pdf}
\Description{Three panels. A: bars of first-pass accuracy per actor model, about equal with and without roles. B: bars of recovery after a wrong first answer per actor model and condition, lower without roles for gemma-4. C: stacked bars of deliberation cycles spent submitting versus revising for gemma-4.}
\caption{\textbf{The phase dissociation, and what it is made of.}
\textbf{(A)} Removing roles leaves first-pass accuracy alone.
\textbf{(B)} The difference appears after the evaluator says the answer is
wrong; enforcement moves recovery back. \textbf{(C)} The same six-cycle
budget, spent differently. Bars are \emph{marginal} rates over each
condition's own first-pass-wrong set; bracketed $p$-values are
\emph{paired}, on a smaller population --- both labelled on the figure.
Pre-registered scoring, bootstrap bars
(\S\S\ref{subsec:recovery},~\ref{subsec:enforce}).}
\label{fig:dissociation}
\end{figure}

That would be the whole result if performance were a single number, but
a team that answers also has to repair.

\paragraph{What a cycle is, and what a team can spend one on.} The pipeline
runs a fixed grid of six deliberation cycles --- three outer
rounds of two inner rounds --- and a cycle is one pass of
Planner~$\rightarrow$~Executor~$\rightarrow$~Reviewer. The grid does not
change: a further cycle runs whether or not the Reviewer approved. What the
Reviewer's verdict decides is whether the cycle ends in a
\emph{submission} or a \emph{revision}. On approval the current answer
goes to the oracle-gated evaluator, which returns PASS --- the trajectory
ends there --- or FAIL with a non-leaking repair hint that becomes the next
cycle's advice; without approval nothing is submitted, no verdict is
consumed, and the next cycle carries the Reviewer's own critique forward
instead. Every trajectory therefore splits its realized cycles into
submissions and revisions, and \emph{that split is the one thing the two
conditions are free to differ in}: the grid, the agents, the budget, the
evaluator and the hint text are identical by construction. The two
conditions have the same number of chances to change their answer and
differ in what each chance is spent on.

\paragraph{Premature stopping, tested and rejected.} The standing
explanation for a team that fails to recover is premature consensus: it
agrees with itself, stops early, and never uses the budget it was given.
We test that and reject it on three observations that point the
same way. Both conditions \emph{hold} six cycles by construction; both
\emph{realize} nearly all of them ($5.52$ versus $5.83$, gemma-4,
first-pass-wrong) with no trajectory cut short; and the role-free
team draws \emph{more} oracle verdicts, not fewer (\S\ref{sec:method}).
That last is decisive, because every rival explanation of the form ``the
role-free team had less to work with'' predicts \emph{fewer} verdicts --- a
verdict being precisely how a team learns its answer is wrong --- and it
draws roughly twice as many and still recovers less. No budget, no stopping
rule and no information asymmetry is left to attribute the gap to.

\textbf{What differs is use} (Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C): the
role-free team converts nearly its whole budget into resubmissions and does
not rework the answer once. The behavioral hinge is the Reviewer's decline:
in $98.1\%$ of gemma-4's role-free first-pass-wrong trajectories the
Reviewer never declines at all, against $5.1\%$ with roles ($61.6\%$ versus
$4.3\%$ for gpt-oss). An approval ends the cycle in a verdict on the answer
the team already has; a decline is the only way a cycle gets spent on
producing a different one.

\paragraph{What the frozen-answer rate measures.} The consequence is an
answer that does not move. We count a trajectory as \emph{frozen} if the
entire transcript --- every role message across all six cycles ---
contains exactly one distinct answer string. Not that the team re-derived
the same value: that it never at any point wrote down a second candidate
to compare against the first. On gemma-4's first-pass-wrong trajectories
that is $0.845$ of the role-free condition against $0.288$ with roles. The
funnel separates the two failure shapes: of $316$ role-structured
first-pass-wrong trajectories $133$ submit a second time and $128$ of
those hold a second distinct answer at some point, while of the $316$
role-free ones \emph{all} submit again and only $49$ ever hold one.
\textbf{The role-free team is not failing to get another turn; it is using
every turn to send back what it already sent.} This mirrors
Degeneration-of-Thought~\cite{liang2024divergent}; the point is the
inversion: multi-agency is the prescribed cure that role-free
multi-agency does not deliver. Rubber-stamping is a symptom of answer
lock-in, not established as its cause: we observe the Reviewer approving
and the answer not moving, and cannot separate a Reviewer that has stopped
objecting from one whose objection reaches a Planner no longer
differentiated enough to act on it. Accumulated context is a real
competing cause of recovery failure~\cite{yang2026contamination} and does
not account for this gap, which survives context matching in the lower
quartiles --- though in gpt-oss's top quartile the ordering
reverses, and that stratification is directional only, since
context is nearly a function of condition (full panel in the supplement).

\subsection{R3. The difference in feedback use becomes a difference in
outcome}
\label{subsec:recovery}

A behavioral difference matters only if an outcome depends on it --- a team
could resubmit mechanically and still finish correct, if the grader
accepted the second showing or if teams only resubmit on problems they
would have failed anyway. They do not. Conditional on the first submission
being graded wrong, the role-free team recovers less often
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}B): gemma-4 falls $0.222 \rightarrow 0.051$
($-0.1709$ $[-0.2247,-0.1171]$), gpt-oss $0.324 \rightarrow 0.247$.
\textbf{Only gemma-4's survives pairing}: on problems first-pass-wrong
under \emph{both} conditions it recovers on $37$ the role-free team misses
against $3$ the other way (exact McNemar $p=2.0\times10^{-8}$).

\textbf{For gemma-4 the recovery gap is the final-accuracy gap.} Every
problem runs under both conditions; both reach a correct first answer on
$1{,}284$ of $1{,}600$, and a correct first answer is never lost, so the
paired final-accuracy difference --- $-54/1{,}600=-0.0338$,
$p=1.6\times10^{-5}$, conditioned on nothing --- equals the difference in
recoveries, $70$ against $16$. By first-pass stratum it is $+34$ on the
$237$ problems both teams miss, a set fixed by both conditions' outcomes,
where the paired test above applies; and $+20$ on the $79+79$ problems
only one team misses ($32$ recovered with roles against $12$ without),
where the two teams condition on different problems and the rates are
descriptive. Both parts favor roles.

\paragraph{Scope across actors.} The recovery result is
clean in gemma-4, directional but unresolved in gpt-oss
(paired $p=0.208$; its first-pass rates also differ by $+0.050$, so the
accounting above does not extend to it), and absent in
Llama-3.3 ($0.014$ versus $0.015$). There the recovery rate does not move but behavior
does --- with roles the team declines and revises yet rarely produces a
second candidate (\S\ref{sec:discussion}) --- and the rate is not a floor,
since enforcement raises it to $0.139$ ($p=3.8\times10^{-6}$,
\S\ref{subsec:enforce}); the test has ${\approx}80\%$ power against an excess of $0.04$--$0.05$ where
gemma-4's effect is $0.171$.

\paragraph{Final accuracy, against grader noise.} Pooled over both phases
the role-free minus role-structured difference is $-0.0338$ for gemma-4,
$+0.0163$ ($p=0.062$) for gpt-oss and $+0.0025$ ($p=1.00$) for Llama-3.3. Repeated identical evaluator prompts at temperature $0$
disagree on $20$ of $2{,}790$ distinct graded strings ($0.0072$, keyed on
exact candidate bytes); rather than divide an effect by a disagreement rate
--- noise largely cancels in a paired contrast --- we propagate it inside
the bootstrap. gemma-4's interval survives ($[-0.0494,-0.0175]$);
gpt-oss's and Llama-3.3's do not, so those are not resolvable
above grader noise rather than absent.

\paragraph{Is the role-free team just a single agent?}
\label{subsec:notsingle}
A standing objection holds that a setup where collapse is free
never needed multi-agent structure at all~\cite{zhang2026rethinking}. This
asks a different question from ours --- whether the team is worth its budget,
not what roles do inside it --- and a budget-matched single-agent comparator
cuts both ways: a collapsed gpt-oss team beats its own single agent
($+0.0294$) while gemma-4's is significantly worse in both
conditions ($-0.0225$, $-0.0563$). Neither ``multi-agent systems are just
single agents'' nor ``more agents help'' survives. The comparator comes from
a previously released corpus whose two actor arms were produced in separate
runs, so each direction is a sound guard and \emph{the gap between
actors is not} (supplement).

\subsection{R4. The same dissociation in a second domain}
\label{subsec:xdomain}

\label{fig:xdomain}
\end{figure}

Everything so far is competition mathematics, where a wrong answer is a
discrete object a team can hold or discard. A domain with free-response
answers and different subject matter tests whether the split belongs to the organization or to the benchmark. Both
halves reappear on SciBench --- the complete $574$-problem
matched slice of free-response college science, $2{,}296$ trajectories,
frozen separately (Figure~\ref{fig:xdomain}). The role-free prompt is
byte-identical to its Omni-MATH form; the role-structured one keeps the
same structural contract with domain wording adapted, as does the
evaluator, which must grade scientific rather than mathematical
equivalence --- so no clause-specific metric crosses domains. For gemma-4 first-pass
accuracy is flat and paired recovery falls $0.212 \rightarrow 0.020$
($n=99$, $p=6.6\times10^{-5}$), not distinguishable in size from
Omni-MATH. gpt-oss's cell is underpowered (power $0.06$--$0.45$
at $n=96$, depending on the test assumed) and resolves nothing; Llama-3.3
was not run.

\subsection{R5. Structural enforcement is consistent with the
interpretation, and
introduces brittleness}
\label{subsec:enforce}

Every result so far comes from taking organization \emph{away}, which alone
cannot distinguish ``role structure supplies revisability'' from ``the role
prompts carried some other useful content we also deleted.'' So we run the
intervention the other way, replacing the request with a constraint:
making the division of labor unavoidable at the interface --- where no
wording can talk its way around it --- should return the capacity to
produce a second distinct candidate. It does. The manipulation check
passes: the planner asserts a final answer in $0.000$ of enforced first
passes in all three actors against $0.979$/$0.898$/$0.889$ under generic
prompts, as in \S\ref{subsec:collapse},
with $838$/$1{,}183$/$395$ role-boundary rejections per cell (formatting
coercions excluded by a definition fixed in advance). Enforcement
\textbf{un-freezes the answer}: $0.119$/$0.092$/$0.072$ against the
role-free $0.845$/$0.208$/$0.806$, and below the role-structured rate
except for gpt-oss. It does not change what the planner \emph{computes},
so an agent can be structurally stopped from answering without being
stopped from doing the work.

Recovery follows for Llama-3.3; for the other two the direction is
consistent but not resolvable against attrition. Recovery is computed on
every trajectory with a recorded incorrect first verdict, including the
$150$ enforced trajectories that received one then died in regeneration ---
all necessarily first-pass wrong, since a passing verdict ends the loop,
and scored $0$ by the preregistration. On that denominator enforcement
recovers at $0.314$/$0.395$/$0.139$ against $0.222$/$0.324$/$0.014$, but
the paired contrast survives only for Llama-3.3 ($p=3.8\times10^{-6}$);
gemma-4 ($p=0.298$) and gpt-oss ($p=0.105$) do not. Conditioning instead on
successful grading would manufacture significance out of the same one-sided
attrition we reject for accuracy below.

\textbf{Improved revisability does not become end-to-end accuracy, because
enforcement is brittle.} Under the pre-registered rule the accuracy
difference is $-0.0225$ / $-0.0050$ / $+0.0175$ (gemma-4 / gpt-oss /
Llama-3.3): one significant negative, two nulls. Dropping the
$8.0\%$/$9.9\%$/$17.0\%$ of enforced trajectories that never finish ---
against $0.0$--$0.7\%$ role-structured and $0.0$--$2.5\%$ role-free ---
gives $+0.0190$ / $+0.0361$ / $+0.0453$: all positive, all significant,
ordered by actor weakness, exactly the published capability-equalizer
direction~\cite{canedo2026architecture}. \textbf{That second reading is
survivorship}: the preregistration fixed in advance that such trajectories
count incorrect, and the benefit appears only after conditioning
on successful parsing. Restoring shared history at fixed enforcement does
not rescue it either --- attrition, not context. Both scorings with
intervals are tabulated in the supplement.

The enforced recovery and first-pass rates do not
compose into that accuracy column --- the never-graded trajectories break
the shared denominator the accounting of \S\ref{subsec:recovery} needs
(supplement).
