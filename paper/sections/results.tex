\subsection{R1. Roles create specialization, but not a better first
answer}
\label{subsec:collapse}

We delete the role clauses and keep the agents, interaction order, budget
and evaluator; the role-free footer also asks every agent for its current
best answer. Functional collapse, the share of first cycles in which every
agent asserts its own final answer, rises from at most $0.120$ with roles to
at least $0.846$ without them for all three actors on Omni-MATH, the
gemma-4, gpt-oss and Llama-3.3 models (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}). Most of the change comes
from the Planner (per-role rates in the supplement). The Executor answers in
both conditions; the Planner seldom answers with roles (at most $0.183$) and nearly always does
without them, so the division of labor rests on one prohibition, its
\textsc{must not} clause. Because the role-free footer asks for an answer,
the absolute level partly reflects that request; the claim concerns the
\emph{change}. The ordering holds for every actor under all three
answer-marker definitions (the cues that count as asserting an answer;
supplement).



\paragraph{Content duplication and a second opinion.} For the gemma-4 and
Llama-3.3 models, the role-free Planners write full solutions before boxing
an answer, and their messages share far more numbers with the Executor's
than with roles, so the Executor redoes the Planner's work (supplement). A
frontier model judging a sample of first cycles against a frozen rubric
agrees with the collapse measure (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}); the judge was
outcome-blind but not condition-blind (supplement), and the collapse claim
does not rest on it.

\paragraph{First-pass accuracy does not follow.} The collapse costs no
accuracy: on problems graded both with roles and without them (the two
role-free conditions are identical until the first verdict), first-pass accuracy is \emph{identical} for gemma-4 ($+0.0000$,
$[-0.0156,+0.0150]$), flat for Llama-3.3 and higher without roles for
gpt-oss ($+0.0503$, $[+0.0339,+0.0666]$; Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}A).
Because one model plays every agent, a Planner that solves the problem
brings no competence the Executor lacks. The gpt-oss model may gain because its
answers vary between derivations (\S\ref{subsec:mechanism}), so extra
independent solutions add chances to be right; we did not test this.

\paragraph{A logging defect.} \label{subsec:logging} Our runner recorded
a response's hidden reasoning trace only when its visible content was
empty, so most of gpt-oss-120b's role-free derivations ($75.7\%$ of
messages) are missing from the record (supplement). Agents pass content
only, in every condition, so the defect is in the record alone and leaves
no point estimate of gpt-oss's duplication. It does not manufacture the
collapse: in first cycles with no affected message ($106$ without roles),
role-free collapse is $0.821$ and role-structured $0.118$.

\subsection{R2. With feedback available to both teams, the role-free team
recovers more}
\label{subsec:recovery}

\paragraph{What a cycle is.} The pipeline runs a fixed grid of six
deliberation cycles, three outer rounds of two inner rounds, each one pass
of Planner, Executor and Reviewer; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows how a
cycle ends in a \emph{submission} or as an \emph{internal cycle} without
submission.

\paragraph{Recovery and final accuracy.} Both teams can obtain the
evaluator's feedback, so this is the main, exploratory comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:main}; \S\ref{subsec:prereg}).
The role-free team recovers more in all five cells. For gemma-4 on
Omni-MATH it recovers $0.609$ of its failed trajectories against $0.222$
(each team over its own; Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}B), and of the $236$
problems both first answer wrongly, $89$ are recovered only without roles
and $4$ only with them ($p=6.2\times10^{-22}$). Both teams reach the same
first-pass rate and never lose a correct first answer, so the extra
recoveries carry straight into final accuracy ($0.921$ against $0.846$).
They are genuine revisions: nearly every passing answer differs from the
one first rejected, and identical evaluator prompts almost never disagree
(supplement). The other two actors repeat the ordering, and the feedback
leaves their first-pass accuracy essentially unchanged, as expected, since the two
role-free conditions are identical until the first verdict.

\paragraph{Leakage and reproducibility.} A hint written with the gold
answer in view could hand it over. Hints contain it verbatim in at most
$2.3\%$ of failing verdicts in any cell, and dropping the gemma-4
trajectories on Omni-MATH that saw one barely moves either recovery rate
(supplement); we did not audit partial leakage. A second, independent run of the two gemma-4 cells with feedback
is close to the first, though the paired test on the SciBench benchmark
weakens from $p=0.013$ to $0.052$; we report the first run throughout
(supplement).

\subsection{R3. The Reviewer's approval governs feedback opportunities}
\label{subsec:mechanism}

Each submission returns a verdict and, on a FAIL, a hint written with the
gold answer in view, so every submission is another chance to revise. The
two teams differ in how often they seize this opportunity.

\paragraph{A gate on submission.} The role-structured Reviewer dictates when
the team submits. For the gemma-4 model, this team submits $1.27$ times per
failed trajectory, compared to $3.20$ for the role-free team
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C), and it submits less often in every cell
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). Much of this withholding occurs before any feedback
exists: $53\%$ of the Reviewer's declines happen before the first
submission, and $84$ of the $316$ failed trajectories never submit, so they
are graded once at the end and never see external feedback. The prompt
clause forbidding approval right after a FAIL covers only $19\%$ of the
declines. In later cycles where approval is allowed, the Reviewer still
declines $75\%$ of the time, against $4\%$ for the role-free team's third
agent. On Omni-MATH the clause covers at most $37\%$ of the declines for
any actor, so there the gate reflects the Reviewer's disposition rather
than the letter of its prompt, plausibly because an agent instructed to
critique will search for flaws; on SciBench the clause covers more
(supplement).

\paragraph{Removing the gate.} If this gate causes the gap, bypassing it
with the role prompts unchanged should eliminate the gap. In a direct,
exploratory test, we kept the role-structured prompts byte for byte but
submitted the team's answer in every cycle, overriding the Reviewer. We ran
it once, for gemma-4 on the $400$ Omni-MATH problems of the post-freeze
re-run (supplement). Every cycle the team uses then becomes a submission
(Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C, bottom row), and recovery surges from
$0.198$ to $0.613$ (paired, $26$ against $2$, $p=3.0\times10^{-6}$),
matching the role-free team's $0.549$ on the same problems ($7$ against
$7$). First-pass accuracy does not move, so the Reviewer's early declines
did not buy a better first answer either. With the role prompts unchanged,
overriding the Reviewer's approval closed the gap in this run.

\paragraph{Matched on verdicts (post hoc).} A descriptive analysis examines
whether the gap persists at an equal number of verdicts. On problems both
teams first answer wrongly, we truncate each role-free trajectory to the
number of verdicts the role-structured team received. No cell then shows a
significant difference (Table~\ref{tab:main}; Llama-3.3's cell has five
events); pooled and stratified by cell, the difference is $+0.009$ in favor
of roles ($95\%$ interval $-0.014$ to $+0.033$). The matching is coarse: a
trajectory cut off after a single FAIL cannot recover, so wherever the
role-structured team received one verdict the pair ties at zero by
construction, as in most of gemma-4's pairs. Restricted to problems with at
least two verdicts, the difference remains not significant; recoding the
scoring defect of \S\ref{sec:limitations} gives $+0.029$ ($+0.004$ to
$+0.054$), a small significant margin for roles (further variants in the
supplement). No variant puts the role-free team significantly ahead. A
truncation cannot show that the two teams use each verdict equally well,
but combined with the forced-submission run it indicates that the gap stems
mainly from how often the team requests feedback, rather than from how
effectively it uses that feedback.

\paragraph{The feedback-withheld diagnostic.} This diagnostic removes
external feedback entirely and reveals what a role ablation measures when
it also removes the feedback channel. The role-free team still submits in
almost every cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation}C), but its agents receive
no signal that the answer failed, so they repeat it: for gemma-4 the team
keeps a single distinct answer in $84.5\%$ of failed trajectories. Adding
the verdict and hint to the identical prompts changes this behavior. The
team explores new answers, and recovery rises from $0.051$ to $0.609$
(paired, $139$ against $2$, $p=7.2\times10^{-39}$). Against this diagnostic
alone, roles would appear load-bearing for repair: strongly for gemma-4
($0.222$ against $0.051$), directionally for gpt-oss ($0.324$ against
$0.247$; $p=0.21$), whose answer moves even without feedback (a single
distinct answer in $20.8\%$ of failed trajectories), and not for Llama-3.3
($0.014$ against $0.015$), whose role-structured team rarely submits.

\subsection{R4. Generality and organizational constraints}
\label{subsec:xdomain}

\paragraph{A second domain.} The SciBench benchmark's complete text-only slice of $574$ free-response
college science problems, frozen separately, tests whether the pattern
belongs to the organization or to competition mathematics. The role-free
prompt is byte-identical across domains; the role-structured and evaluator
prompts adapt only their domain wording (supplement). For both actors run
there, the gemma-4 and gpt-oss models, the ordering returns
(Table~\ref{tab:main}): first-pass accuracy is flat, recovery is highest
with feedback and no roles, and so is final accuracy. The
recovery contrast is weaker, consistent with a looser gate: the
role-structured team almost always submits at least once (all but $3$ and
$1$ of its failed trajectories), so more of the feedback reaches it, and,
as on Omni-MATH, the contrast vanishes when both teams are credited with
the same number of verdicts (answer-lock rates in the supplement).

\paragraph{Structural enforcement.} \label{subsec:enforce} Enforced and role-structured prompts carry the same advice slot, so this
contrast is matched on feedback access. Enforcement replaces the behavioral
request with a constraint: the Planner's final answer becomes
unrepresentable. The manipulation check passes: the Planner asserts a final
answer in $0.000$ of enforced first cycles for all three actors, against at
least $0.889$ without roles. Enforcement does not change what the Planner
\emph{computes}, so the adjudication still finds no functional division in
$0.071$ to $0.188$ of enforced first cycles: an agent can be structurally
stopped from answering without being stopped from doing the work. It does
loosen the gate: the enforced team submits more often for every actor and,
with more hints arriving, unlocks the answer for gemma-4 and Llama-3.3
(supplement).

Recovery rises most where the gate was tightest. Llama-3.3's
role-structured team almost never submits, and under enforcement its
recovery rises about tenfold, from $0.014$ to $0.139$
($p=3.8\times10^{-6}$), close to the role-free team's $0.147$. For gemma-4
and gpt-oss the direction is the same ($p=0.30$ and $0.11$) but not
resolvable against attrition: enforced trajectories that died after an
incorrect first verdict count as unrecovered, as the pre-registration
requires.

Improved revisability does not become end-to-end accuracy, because
enforcement is brittle. A malformed or boundary-violating output must be
regenerated, and when the regeneration budget runs out the trajectory dies:
$8.0\%$, $9.9\%$ and $17.0\%$ of enforced trajectories never finish,
against at most $2.5\%$ in any other condition, and the weakest actor
breaks the schema most often (supplement). Under the pre-registered rule,
which counts these trajectories as incorrect, enforcement changes accuracy
by $-0.0225$, $-0.0050$ and $+0.0175$ for gemma-4, gpt-oss and Llama-3.3:
one significant negative and two nulls. Dropping them instead gives gains
for all three actors, all significant and ordered by actor weakness,
matching the direction reported for the capability
equalizer~\cite{canedo2026architecture}. That reading is survivorship bias,
since it scores only the trajectories that finished. Restoring the shared
history at fixed enforcement helps gemma-4, mostly through fewer
trajectories dying, and not gpt-oss (supplement).
