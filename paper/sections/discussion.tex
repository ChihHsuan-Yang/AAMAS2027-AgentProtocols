\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a role diagram is evidence of.} Both interventions changed
the realized organization dramatically and final accuracy barely at all, in
opposite directions. A diagram is a hypothesis about a system, and
task-outcome evaluation --- the only evaluation most multi-agent systems
receive --- cannot test it. The phase decomposition separates ``the roles are
working'' from ``the system is accurate'': an aggregate score averages over a
stage where the organization is idle and one where it does all its work.
Capability-dependence accounts predict a single ordering, structure helping
weaker models and mattering less as models
improve~\cite{canedo2026architecture,yu2026bottleneck,dochkina2026drop}. Our
panel does not order that way, and the mechanism says why: what roles supply
is not scaffolding for a weak solver but a second distinct candidate after a
failure. Our weakest actor is the one it does not reach --- not for want of a
repair channel, since enforcement opens that channel in the same actor, but
because role labels do not open it there.

\paragraph{What our own measurement errors imply.} Three instruments in this
study reported confidently on things they could not see. An
exact-duplication metric returned $0.000$ for actors a model judge found
collapsed, because their agents paraphrase rather than copy; a logging defect
reduced one actor's planner to $37$ visible characters, which we nearly
reported as a behavior before its $2.85$ characters per second showed the
work had been done and discarded; and the adjudication built to check the
first of these leaked the condition, its neutralized role tokens surviving in
every Arm~A item and almost no Arm~B one. Any claim that a system's structure
is intact should state what the instrument would have to see to report
otherwise --- and a blinding claim should be tested on the bytes the judge
saw, not on the substitution applied to them.
