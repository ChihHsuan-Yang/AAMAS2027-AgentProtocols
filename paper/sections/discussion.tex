\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why organization would be phase-specific.} The two phases
demand different things. Producing a first answer is a \emph{generation}
problem: there is no incumbent candidate, and three agents arriving at the
same place by three routes costs nothing but tokens, since the pipeline
emits one answer and it is as good as the best route that reached it. A
division of labor has nothing to do here that redundancy does not already
do. Repairing a wrong answer is a \emph{differentiation} problem: an
incumbent candidate exists, it is in every agent's context, and the useful
move is to produce something unlike it. The failure mode is then not
insufficient effort --- the role-free team spends its whole budget --- but
insufficient difference, as six cycles of agents who are all now the same
solver, all conditioned on the same rejected answer, reliably produce that
answer again. What a role contract supplies is a reason for one agent's
next output not to be a function of the previous agent's: worth nothing
when there is nothing to differ from, a great deal when there is. It also
explains why the clause that matters is a \textsc{must not}, and why
enforcement --- the same prohibition moved to the interface --- moves the
same quantity.

\paragraph{What a role diagram is evidence of.} Both interventions changed
the realized organization dramatically and final accuracy barely at all,
in opposite directions. A diagram is a hypothesis about a system, and
task-outcome evaluation --- the only evaluation most multi-agent systems
receive --- cannot test it: an aggregate score averages a stage where the
organization is idle with one where it does all its work, dominated by
whichever stage has more mass. In our two stronger actors most problems
are answered on the first pass ($0.802$ and $0.710$), so most of each
accuracy number is generation, precisely the stage where the organization
contributes nothing. A system can therefore lose its entire organization
and move the headline metric by $0.0338$. Ours did. Two cheap additions
fix most of it: report performance \emph{conditioned on the first attempt
having failed} --- the split that separates our two phases, computable
from logs any retry-capable system already writes --- and \emph{measure}
the realized organization instead of assuming the specified one. A
collapsed organization and a load-bearing one are indistinguishable by
accuracy and trivially distinguishable by whether all three agents are
writing down answers.

Capability-dependence accounts predict structure helping weaker models and
mattering less as they
improve~\cite{canedo2026architecture,yu2026bottleneck,dochkina2026drop};
our panel does not order that way, and the mechanism says why. If roles
supply differentiation after failure rather than scaffolding for a weak
solver, the actors they help are those with a repair channel to open, not
those with the lowest accuracy --- and our weakest actor is the one role
labels do not reach, though enforcement opens that channel in it.
Capability is the wrong axis; whether the organization can produce a
second distinct candidate is the right one.

\paragraph{What our own measurement errors imply.} Three instruments here
reported confidently on what they could not see: an exact-duplication
metric returned $0.000$ for actors a model judge found collapsed; a
logging defect reduced one actor's planner to $37$ visible characters,
which we nearly reported as a behavior; and the adjudication built to
check the first leaked the condition it was meant to be blind to. Each
produced a clean, plausible reading while not measuring the thing. A claim
that a system's structure is intact should therefore state what the
instrument would have to see to report otherwise --- and a blinding claim
should be tested on the bytes the judge saw, not the substitution applied
to them.
