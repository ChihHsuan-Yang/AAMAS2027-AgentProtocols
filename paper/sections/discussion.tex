\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What roles do after feedback.} Producing a first answer is a
\emph{generation} problem: three agents reaching the same place by three
routes cost nothing but tokens, and first-pass accuracy is nearly
indifferent to the division of labor. After a failure the team must produce
something unlike an incumbent candidate, and, gpt-oss aside, it rarely
does so unless it knows the candidate failed. Given that knowledge, the role contract adds
little capacity to revise: verdict for verdict, the role-free team is no
better. What the contract decides is when the team asks: the
role-structured Reviewer withholds approval until it is satisfied, so the
team draws fewer verdicts, and sometimes no feedback at all; overriding
the Reviewer closed the gap for gemma-4. Under a ground-truth
evaluator and a fixed cycle budget, fewer verdicts mean fewer recoveries.
Where verification is costly, noisy or unavailable, the usual case outside
a benchmark, a team that asks less could be the better design; this
experiment cannot say. In the terms of \S\ref{sec:related}, the gate lives
in the deontic part of the role contract --- when the Reviewer may approve
--- and deleting the roles deleted it together with the functional
assignment.

\paragraph{What a builder can take from this.} Two things. With a
cheap, reliable verifier, a Reviewer role that withholds submission costs
recovery; let the team submit, or gate on the verifier rather than on the
critic. And whatever the roles, route
the verifier's feedback to every agent that can act on it: in our pipeline
the gemma-4 and Llama-3.3 teams without that slot almost never recovered,
and the same prompts with it recovered most.

\paragraph{What a role ablation removes.} In a prompted multi-agent system
the channels through which agents receive feedback, tool output or a
verifier's verdict are slots in the same templates that carry the roles.
Rewriting the templates to remove the roles can silently remove a channel
with them: nothing in the budget record, the call counts or the accuracy
reports its absence. A role ablation therefore needs an
information audit beside the budget audit --- for each agent and
condition, the set of runtime inputs its template actually consumes --- and
a contrast is matched only where those sets agree. Ours differ in one
input, the feedback, which is why the role-free condition is reported both
without and with it.

\paragraph{What a role diagram is evidence of.} Precise reviewing need
not mean critique is taken up~\cite{yang2026precise}, and a clean division
of labor need not mean a team adapts; both are intermediate properties, and
what matters is whether corrective information changes the next action. A
diagram is a hypothesis, and task-outcome evaluation --- the only
evaluation most multi-agent systems receive --- cannot test it: an
aggregate score averages a stage where the organization is idle with one
where it does all its work. Our gemma-4 team lost its division of labor and
moved final accuracy by $-0.034$ with the feedback withheld and $+0.075$
with it, both almost entirely after a first answer had failed. Two cheap
additions would have exposed this: report performance \emph{conditioned on
the first attempt having failed}, and \emph{measure} the realized organization rather
than assume the specified one.
