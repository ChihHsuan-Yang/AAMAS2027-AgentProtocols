\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why organization would be phase-specific.} The central result is
that one manipulation has two different prices depending on when it is
charged, and that is worth an argument rather than a restatement. Consider
what each phase actually demands. Producing a first answer is a
\emph{generation} problem: there is no incumbent candidate, every agent is
working from the same problem statement, and three agents arriving at the
same place by three routes costs nothing but tokens --- the pipeline still
emits one answer, and it is as good as the best route that reached it. A
division of labor has nothing to do here that redundancy does not already
do. Repairing a wrong answer is a \emph{differentiation} problem. An
incumbent candidate exists, it is in every agent's context, and the useful
move is to produce something unlike it. The failure mode is not insufficient
effort --- the role-free team spends its whole budget --- but insufficient
difference: six cycles of agents who are all now the same solver, all
conditioned on the same rejected answer, reliably produce that answer again.
What a role contract supplies is a reason for one agent's next output not to
be a function of the previous agent's, and that is worth nothing when there
is nothing to differ from and a great deal when there is.

This also explains why the clause that matters is a prohibition. The
\textsc{must not} clauses --- the Planner does not state a final answer, the
Reviewer critiques rather than re-derives --- are what keep two agents from
occupying the same position, and they are exactly the clauses with no
effect on first-pass output and a large one on whether a second candidate
can exist. Enforcement is the same prohibition moved from prose to the
interface, and it moves the same quantity: the frozen-answer rate falls to
$0.119$/$0.092$/$0.072$, below the role-free rate in every actor and below
the role-structured rate in two of three.

\paragraph{What a role diagram is evidence of.} Both interventions changed
the realized organization dramatically and final accuracy barely at all, in
opposite directions. A diagram is a hypothesis about a system, and
task-outcome evaluation --- the only evaluation most multi-agent systems
receive --- cannot test it: an aggregate score averages a stage where the
organization is idle with one where it does all its work, and the average is
dominated by whichever stage has more mass. In our two stronger actors most
problems are answered on the first pass ($0.802$ and $0.710$), so most of
each accuracy number is generation --- precisely the stage where the
organization contributes nothing. A system can therefore lose its entire
organization and move the headline metric by $0.0338$. Ours did.

\paragraph{What this asks of an evaluation.} The practical consequence is
that an aggregate score is the wrong instrument for an architectural claim,
and two cheap additions fix most of it. First, report performance
\emph{conditioned on the first attempt having failed}, not only pooled:
that single split is what separates our two phases, and it is computable
from logs any retry-capable system already writes. Second, measure the
realized organization rather than assuming the specified one --- our
functional-collapse measure is one answer-marker pattern applied to the
three messages of the first pipeline pass. A system whose organization has silently collapsed and one
whose organization is load-bearing are not distinguishable by accuracy, but
they are trivially distinguishable by whether all three agents are writing
down answers.

Capability-dependence accounts predict structure helping weaker models and
mattering less as they
improve~\cite{canedo2026architecture,yu2026bottleneck,dochkina2026drop}; our
panel does not order that way, and the mechanism says why. If what roles
supply is differentiation after failure rather than scaffolding for a weak
solver, the actors it helps are those with a repair channel to open, not
those with the lowest accuracy --- and our weakest actor is the one role
labels do not reach, though enforcement opens that channel in the same
actor. Capability is the wrong axis; whether the organization can produce a
second distinct candidate is the right one.

\paragraph{What our own measurement errors imply.} Three instruments here
reported confidently on what they could not see: an exact-duplication metric
returned $0.000$ for actors a model judge found collapsed; a logging defect
reduced one actor's planner to $37$ visible characters, which we nearly
reported as a behavior; and the adjudication built to check the first leaked
the condition it was meant to be blind to. The shape is the same in all
three: each instrument had a way of producing a clean, plausible reading
when it was in fact not measuring the thing. Any claim that a system's
structure is intact should therefore state what the instrument would have to
see to report otherwise --- and a blinding claim should be tested on the
bytes the judge saw, not the substitution applied to them.
