\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What roles do after feedback.} Producing a first answer is a
\emph{generation} problem: three agents reaching the same place by three
routes cost nothing but tokens, a division of labor has nothing to do that
redundancy does not already do, and first-pass accuracy is indifferent to
it in every actor. After a failure the problem changes --- the team must
produce something unlike an incumbent candidate --- and it can do so only
if it knows the candidate failed. Given that knowledge, the role contract
does not supply the capacity to revise; the role-free team has it. What the
contract decides is how a fixed budget is split between two uses of a
verdict: internal review before the next submission, or the next
submission itself. The role-structured contract buys review --- its
resubmissions pass more often, and each verdict after the first yields
more recoveries --- and the role-free team buys verdicts. Under an exact
oracle and a six-verdict cap, verdicts win. Where verification is costly,
noisy or unavailable, the usual case outside a benchmark, the same contract
could be the better allocation; this design cannot say which.

\paragraph{What a role ablation removes.} In a prompted multi-agent system
the channels through which agents receive feedback, tool output or a
verifier's verdict are slots in the same templates that carry the roles.
Rewriting the templates to remove the roles can remove a channel with
them, and silently: a runtime input a template does not reference is
simply not shown, and nothing in the budget record, the call counts or the
accuracy reports its absence. A role ablation therefore needs an
information audit beside the budget audit --- for each agent and
condition, the set of runtime inputs its template actually consumes --- and
a contrast is matched only where those sets agree. Ours differ in exactly
one input, the feedback, which is why the role-free condition is reported
both without and with it.

\paragraph{What a role diagram is evidence of.} Precise reviewing need
not mean critique is taken up~\cite{yang2026precise}, and a clean division
of labor need not mean a team adapts; both are intermediate properties, and
what matters is whether corrective information changes the next action. A
diagram is a hypothesis, and task-outcome evaluation --- the only evaluation most
multi-agent systems receive --- cannot test it: an aggregate score averages
a stage where the organization is idle with one where it does all its work,
dominated by whichever has more mass. Our system lost its entire
organization and moved the headline metric by $-0.034$ with the feedback
withheld and $+0.075$ with it, both entirely after a first answer had failed. Two cheap additions
fix most of it: report performance \emph{conditioned on the first attempt
having failed}, computable from logs any retry-capable system already
writes, and \emph{measure} the realized organization rather than assume the
specified one --- indistinguishable by accuracy, trivially distinguishable
by whether all three agents are writing down answers.

\paragraph{What our own measurement errors imply.} Three instruments here
reported confidently on what they could not see: an exact-duplication
metric returned $0.000$ for actors a model judge found collapsed; a
logging defect reduced one actor's planner to $37$ visible characters,
which we nearly reported as a behavior; and the adjudication built to
check the first leaked the condition it was meant to be blind to. Each
produced a clean, plausible reading while not measuring the thing. A claim
that a system's structure is intact should therefore state what the
instrument would have to see to report otherwise --- and a blinding claim
should be tested on the bytes the judge saw, not the substitution applied
to them.
