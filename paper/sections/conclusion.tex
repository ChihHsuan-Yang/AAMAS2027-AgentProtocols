\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Organization and multi-agent computation turn out to be separable, and
measuring the organization does not tell you which carries the load. Deleting
every role instruction destroys the functional division of labor in all three
actors tested ($0.846$--$0.975$ of first pipeline passes become three
independent solvers), and one loses accuracy while the others do not;
enforcing the organization architecturally completes the picture from the
other side, driving answer-authorship compliance to $1.000$ while accuracy
moves nowhere and our pre-registered primary test is null. Two guards keep
this from overreaching: a collapsed team still beats its budget-matched
single agent ($+0.0247$), and the actor that loses accuracy loses 3 points
--- refuting ``multi-agent systems are just single agents'' and ``roles do
not matter'' alike, from our own data. The defensible claim is narrower: role
\emph{differentiation} is load-bearing for one actor and redundant
\emph{computation} for another, role removal leaving the vulnerable actor's
first-submission rate unchanged while costing it recovery.

The measurement lesson travels with the result. \textbf{Whether an
organization is load-bearing cannot be read off how much visible
differentiation an intervention destroys}: it has to be measured on the
outcome, with an instrument shown to see the failure it is meant to detect.
