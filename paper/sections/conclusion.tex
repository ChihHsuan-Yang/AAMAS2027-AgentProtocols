\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when multi-agent organization is load-bearing --- when removing or
altering it degrades performance under matched compute and information
budgets. Holding agents, calls, context policy, verifier, and grading fixed
and varying only what each agent is told it is, organization and multi-agent
computation turn out to be separable, and which of them carries the load
depends on the actor. For the more capable actor, deleting every role
instruction destroys the visible organization --- $69.8\%$ of first pipeline
passes collapse to byte-identical outputs --- at no accuracy cost, on hard
problems as much as easy ones. For the less capable actor the same deletion
produces no first-pass collapse and costs accuracy anyway. Restoring the
organization architecturally completes the picture from the other side:
answer-authorship compliance reaches $1.000$, collapse ${\approx}0$, and
accuracy nowhere, with our pre-registered primary test null.

Two guards keep this from overreaching. A collapsed team still beats its
budget-matched single agent ($+0.0248$), so ``multi-agent systems are just
single agents'' is refuted by our own data; and the actor that loses accuracy
loses 3 points, so ``roles do not matter'' is refuted too. The defensible
claim is narrower: role \emph{differentiation} is load-bearing for one actor
and parallel \emph{computation} for the other. The mechanism says where the
load sits --- role removal leaves the vulnerable actor's first-pass solve
rate unchanged and destroys its recovery ($0.490 \rightarrow 0.045$), because
without roles the team reaches a unanimity it cannot escape. Whether an
organization is load-bearing is therefore not a property of the organization,
but of the organization--actor pair, and must be measured on both sides.
