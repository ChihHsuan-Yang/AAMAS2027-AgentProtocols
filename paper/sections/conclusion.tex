\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when multi-agent organization is load-bearing --- when removing or
altering it degrades performance under matched compute and information
budgets. Holding agents, calls, context policy, verifier, and grading fixed
and varying only what each agent is told it is, organization and multi-agent
computation turn out to be separable, and measuring the organization does not
tell you which of them carries the load. Deleting every role instruction
destroys the functional division of labor in all three actors we tested
($0.845$--$0.975$ of first pipeline passes become three independent solvers),
and one of them loses accuracy while the others do not. Restoring the
organization architecturally completes the picture from the other side:
answer-authorship compliance reaches $1.000$, and accuracy moves nowhere,
with our pre-registered primary test null.

Two guards keep this from overreaching. A collapsed team still beats its
budget-matched single agent ($+0.0247$), so ``multi-agent systems are just
single agents'' is refuted by our own data; and the actor that loses accuracy
loses 3 points, so ``roles do not matter'' is refuted too. The defensible
claim is narrower: role \emph{differentiation} is load-bearing for one actor
and redundant \emph{computation} for another. The mechanism says where the
load sits --- role removal leaves the vulnerable actor's first-submission
rate unchanged and costs it recovery, because without roles the team reaches
a unanimity it cannot escape.

The measurement lesson travels with the result. Our first reading of this
experiment came from an exact-duplication metric, which reported that one
actor collapsed and another did not; a blinded adjudication showed the
second actor collapsing just as thoroughly in language the metric could not
see. \textbf{Whether an organization is load-bearing is not a property of the
organization alone, and cannot be read off how much visible differentiation
an intervention destroys.} It has to be measured on the outcome, on both
sides, with an instrument that has been shown to see the failure it is
supposed to detect.
