\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when multi-agent organization is load-bearing --- when removing or
altering it degrades performance under matched compute and information
budgets. Holding agents, calls, context policy, verifier, and grading fixed
and varying only what each agent is told it is, organization and multi-agent
computation turn out to be separable, and measuring the organization does not
tell you which of them carries the load. Deleting every role instruction
destroys the functional division of labor in all three actors we tested
($0.845$--$0.975$ of first pipeline passes become three independent solvers),
and one of them loses accuracy while the others do not. Restoring the
organization architecturally completes the picture from the other side:
answer-authorship compliance reaches $1.000$, and accuracy moves nowhere,
with our pre-registered primary test null.

Two guards keep this from overreaching. A collapsed team still beats its
budget-matched single agent ($+0.0247$), refuting ``multi-agent systems are
just single agents'' from our own data; and the actor that loses accuracy
loses 3 points, refuting ``roles do not matter'' too. The defensible claim is
narrower: role \emph{differentiation} is load-bearing for one actor and
redundant \emph{computation} for another, and role removal leaves the
vulnerable actor's first-submission rate unchanged while costing it recovery,
because without roles the team reaches a unanimity it cannot escape.

The measurement lesson travels with the result. Our first reading came from
an exact-duplication metric reporting that one actor collapsed and another
did not; a blinded adjudication showed the second collapsing just as
thoroughly, in language the metric could not see. \textbf{Whether an
organization is load-bearing cannot be read off how much visible
differentiation an intervention destroys.} It has to be measured on the
outcome, with an instrument shown to see the failure it is meant to detect.
