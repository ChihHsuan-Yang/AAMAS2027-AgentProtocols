\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Role conditioning is prompt conditioning.}
Orogat et al.~\cite{orogat2026architectural} study nine multi-agent frameworks
as data-intensive systems, holding the LLM fixed and varying architecture along
orchestration, memory, planning interface, specialization, and topology. Their
specialization experiment (Table~14 of v2; Table~12 in v1) crosses four
conditioning strategies with five role labels and finds that role conditioning
does not induce the intended specialization. Two properties of that result bound
what it settles. First, conditioning is applied \emph{in the prompt}, including
the strongest ``expert-guided'' arm, which is a numbered prose recipe; no
condition removes a field, constrains decoding, or restricts context. Second,
the specialization study runs one model (GPT-4o-mini), and the authors note the
effect may differ for models with stronger instruction following. That paper
does test structure~$\times$~actor elsewhere: its \S4.1.3 evaluates
schema-constrained \emph{planning output} across eight models and reports a
clean interaction, with one 7B model violating the schema in 84.7\% of cases
while frontier models never violate. We take care to distinguish the two
constraints. A schema on an agent's \emph{output format} regulates how one
agent's message is shaped; the constraint studied here regulates \emph{which
agent may do which part of the work}, and is asymmetric across agents by
construction. Conflating them is the most likely misreading of this paper, and
\S\ref{sec:method} states the difference in a form a reader can check.

\paragraph{Structure as a substitute for capability.}
Canedo~\cite{canedo2026architecture} is the closest published result to the
interaction we test. Five informationally equivalent specification formats --
prose, Mermaid with ADRs, OpenAPI, C4/Structurizr DSL, and TypeScript interface
contracts -- are given to one coding agent across six models from three vendor
families over 90 trials. The reported format~$\times$~model interaction is
strong: quality spreads of 0.17--0.92 on the strongest models against 0.83--2.42
on weaker ones, supporting the thesis that structured architecture
specifications act as a capability equalizer whose value falls as model strength
rises. We position against this explicitly rather than by vocabulary. Three
differences are substantive. (i)~The constrained object differs: there the
\emph{input specification} handed to an agent, here the \emph{output space} of
each agent, with a field made unrepresentable. (ii)~The constraint there is
symmetric and applies to a single agent; ours is asymmetric \emph{by role} and
is a property of a relation between agents, which is why it has no single-agent
analogue. (iii)~That study measures outcome and infers that structure
compensated for capability; it never measures whether agent behavior changed.
We measure the putative mediator directly. Most importantly, the two accounts
make opposing predictions in a panel where capability and realized fidelity are
anti-correlated (\S\ref{sec:intro}), which is what makes the distinction
empirical rather than terminological. If the benefit tracks capability instead,
we report that we have replicated~\cite{canedo2026architecture} in a multi-agent
setting.

\paragraph{Role differentiation that fails to materialize.}
Dylan et al.~\cite{dylan2026equal} hold total LLM calls fixed and evolve a
Planner--Executor--Critic team as three role prompts over a shared frozen 7B
backbone. The team's best mean score is not significantly better than the single
agent ($0.769$ vs.\ $0.754$, $p = 0.80$) despite $1.8\times$ the evaluation
calls, and a leave-one-in analysis attributes the entire benefit to the
executor, with planner and critic evolving toward empty or low-impact prompts.
The result is a collapse of role differentiation, and it is the paper whose
question is nearest ours. Its scope is also narrow in exactly the directions we
extend: one frozen backbone, roles defined as prompts (a role is a backbone
paired with a natural-language role prompt), and an explicit refusal to claim
that the conclusion transfers to larger or heterogeneous backbones. That
declined generalization -- scale and per-role heterogeneity, named there as
future work -- is the axis this paper varies. We also adopt its behavioral
instruments, influence rate and restate rate, for comparability.

\paragraph{Role violation as a named failure mode.}
Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025why} annotate 1{,}600+ traces across seven
multi-agent frameworks and derive MAST, a 14-mode failure taxonomy grouped into
system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, with strong
inter-annotator agreement ($\kappa = 0.88$). Mode FM-1.2, ``Disobey role
specification,'' is the qualitative category our fidelity measures quantify.
That role violation occurs is therefore established; what is not established is
its rate per contract clause, its dependence on the actor under an identical
prompt, or whether removing it changes anything. Cao et al.~\cite{cao2026modules}
give the complementary training-time account, naming \emph{role drift} as a
failure in which modules preserve or improve end-task scores while taking
role-violating shortcuts invisible to system-level evaluation -- a decomposer
planting answers in its sub-questions, a reader falling back on parametric
memory -- and find that 86\% of one pipeline's apparent RL improvement vanishes
once the decomposer is held to its role. Their remedy, Role Anchor, is a
regularizer applied during end-to-end training. Ours is an interface constraint
at inference on a frozen model; the two address the same phenomenon at different
points in the stack, and their finding that terminal accuracy overstates genuine
role learning is direct support for measuring behavior alongside outcome.

\paragraph{Architectural enforcement of role scope.}
The nearest architectural enforcement is in governance rather than reasoning
performance. Stettler et al.~\cite{stettler2026progressive} package capabilities
into roles and deliver role-scoped tools through a single MCP server that
enforces scope deterministically, on the premise that prompt-defined policies
are probabilistic advice rather than hard constraints -- the same premise as
Arm~C here. Their claim is least-privilege access control; they make no claim
about division of labor or task effectiveness, and no field is removed from a
role's output space to create a functional division. Ge and
Guo~\cite{ge2026roles} enforce five structural contracts in SERO, but those
contracts gate which \emph{edits to a role pool} may be committed during
evolution; the behavioral payload of an evolved role remains a system prompt,
and no agent is structurally prevented from emitting an answer. Barrak et
al.~\cite{barrak2025traceability} evaluate Planner--Executor--Critic pipelines
across three LLMs with structured, blame-attributable handoffs and report
configuration-dependent deltas (from $+36.22$ to $-0.78$) -- an
organization~$\times$~actor interaction present in the data but never named or
tested as such; the handoff there is a validated message format, not a
restriction of output space.

\paragraph{Studies of the same trace corpus.}
Two prior studies analyze the trajectory corpus this work instruments. Jiang et
al.~\cite{jiang2026confidence} audit a confidence-routed broadcast protocol,
separating routing, calibration, and commitment, and conclude those three must
be measured separately before confidence is used for deployment decisions. Yang
et al.~\cite{yang2026protocol} study cost-aware routing \emph{across} protocols,
finding that a pre-collaboration probe predicts baseline failure well
($\mathrm{AUROC} = 0.885$) but predicts protocol-specific value poorly. Both are
observational, both take the trajectory or the poll as the unit, and neither
measures role behavior or intervenes on the organization. The object here --
whether a specified division of labor is realized, and what happens when it is
enforced -- is not addressed by either, and the controlled arms of
\S\ref{sec:method} do not exist in that corpus.
