\section*{What this document is}

The main paper states every claim it makes and is complete without this
file. What it cannot carry, at eight pages, is the full numeric backing
behind some of its sentences. That backing is here.

Every table below is \textbf{generated} by a script from hash-manifested
data --- the frozen snapshot, or the separately manifested post-freeze runs
(feedback-matched, forced submission) --- except the single-agent comparator
(Table~\ref{tab:singleagent}), which comes from a previously released corpus
with no manifest here. No number in a table is hand-typed. Each table
carries its own provenance header in the source, and the tables are
generated by the same scripts from the same outputs as the main paper's, so
a number cannot drift between the two.

\paragraph{Reading the denominators.} Two populations recur and are not
interchangeable. The \emph{marginal} population is every trajectory in a
cell, with each condition on its own denominator. The \emph{paired}
population is only those problems first-pass wrong under \emph{both}
conditions, on one shared denominator; that is the actual test of recovery.
Recovery $p$ values are exact McNemar on this population; first-pass and
final-accuracy $p$ values are exact McNemar on all problems, paired
(as-graded contrasts on the problems graded under both); the
pre-registered P4 contrasts use a bootstrap. Where a table shows both
populations, they are labeled and must not be read across.

\paragraph{Recovery denominator.} Throughout, recovery is conditioned on a
recorded \emph{first} evaluator verdict of incorrect --- including
trajectories that later died in regeneration, which score as failures under
the pre-registered rule. The choice matters, and it is stated here so that
a reader recomputing from the trajectories gets the same denominator.

\section{The pre-registered endpoints in full}

The main paper reports, in its Intervention section, that the pre-registered
primary endpoint P4 --- whether enforcement's effect on accuracy differs by
actor --- is null: none of the three pairwise difference-in-differences
survives Holm; before correction the Llama-3.3$-$gemma-4 interval only just
excludes zero, and a separate resample gives $p=0.055$. It also reports that the \Pone{} manipulation
objective failed. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} is
the complete endpoint table promised there: all five endpoints as written
before any intervention data existed, what each one returned, and the
Holm-corrected verdict.

Two things in it deserve to be read rather than skimmed. First, only one of
the three P4 contrasts is an \emph{informative} null: the two involving
Llama-3.3 cannot detect an interaction of any plausible size at their
sample size, and are uninformative rather than evidence of absence.
Second, the contrast this paper is organized around --- role-structured
versus role-free --- was pre-registered as \textbf{secondary and
exploratory}, and the feedback-matched role-free condition and the
forced-submission run are outside the pre-registration (the feedback-matched
condition's design and reading were committed in writing before its data
existed). All are labeled exploratory in the main paper and here.

\section{First pass and recovery, both populations}

Table~\ref{tab:recovery} gives the frozen comparison between the
role-structured team and the role-free team with the feedback withheld, in
both populations, so that the marginal rates and the paired test can be
checked against each other. (The feedback-matched comparison is in the main
paper's Table~2.)

For gemma-4 the accounting closes exactly. Both conditions
reach a correct first answer on $1{,}284$ of $1{,}600$ problems, and a
correct first answer is never lost (the loop halts on a passing verdict),
so the paired final-accuracy difference, role-free minus role-structured
--- $-54/1{,}600=-0.0338$, exact McNemar $p=1.6\times10^{-5}$, conditioned
on nothing --- equals the difference in recovery counts, $16$ minus $70$.
Stratified by the pair of first-pass outcomes, counting role-structured
minus role-free final correct answers (so the strata sum to $+54$): the $1{,}205$ problems both teams answer correctly first
contribute $0$; the $237$ both miss contribute $38-4=+34$ (discordant
$37$ versus $3$); the $79$ only the
role-structured team misses contribute $32-79=-47$; and the $79$ only the
role-free team misses contribute $79-12=+67$. The last two strata
condition on different problems, so their recovery rates ($32/79$ versus
$12/79$) are descriptive; together they contribute $+20$, in the same
direction as the paired stratum. This accounting needs equal first-pass
counts and does \emph{not} transfer to the other two actors.

\section{Cross-domain replication}

Table~\ref{tab:scibench} is the SciBench replication in full. The main
paper carries both actors in Figure~2 and Table~2; this is the frozen
comparison with its denominators.

The domain contract is \emph{not} byte-identical to Omni-MATH, and the
prompt files in the supplementary material make the difference checkable:
of the $16$ prompts the two domains share, $11$ are byte-identical and $5$
differ. The five are four of the six role-structured prompts (the Planner,
Executor and Reviewer preambles and the Reviewer's routing rules) and the
\textbf{evaluator}'s preamble, which must ask for scientific rather than
mathematical equivalence. \textbf{Both role-free prompts are byte-identical
across domains}, as are the six structurally enforced ones (enforcement was
not run on SciBench).

That asymmetry is worth stating plainly rather than leaving to a diff: the
role-structured prompts, whose team recovers less than the feedback-matched
role-free team, are the ones that were rewritten, and the role-free prompts ran identical text in both domains.
The rewrite is domain wording --- ``algebraic manipulation or
numerical calculation'' where Omni-MATH said ``symbolic manipulation or
arithmetic'' --- and the structural clauses are unchanged. A reader who
suspects the adaptation is doing the work can check the files.

Topology, budget, decoding, retry policy and the evaluator protocol are
unchanged. This is a replication of the \emph{mechanism} under a matched
protocol, not a rerun of identical prompts.

\section{The feedback-matched control, run twice}
\label{sec:bfreplication}

The two gemma-4 cells of the feedback-matched role-free condition were run
twice, independently, about thirteen hours apart, with byte-identical runner
and prompt code; every other feedback-matched cell was run once. Each run
also repeated the drift check of the main text on the same $400$ frozen
problems: the role-structured re-run reaches final accuracy $0.818$ in the
first and $0.845$ in the second, against $0.825$ frozen (paired $p=0.75$ and
$p=0.20$). The main text reports the first run throughout. Table~\ref{tab:bfreplication} gives
both against the frozen role-structured cell. The ordering and the
final-accuracy contrasts reproduce in both domains; the SciBench paired
recovery test, on $100$ problems, is $p=0.013$ in the first run and
$p=0.052$ in the second.

\section{Where the role-structured Reviewer declines}
\label{sec:gate}

The main paper's account of what roles do after feedback --- a gate on
submission --- rests on Table~\ref{tab:gate}, computed from the logged route
of every Reviewer turn. A cycle ends in an approval (a submission) or a
decline. Declines are split by when they happen: before the first
submission, when no feedback exists; in a cycle that received a FAIL, the
only cycle the role-structured clause covers; and in later cycles in which
no clause forbids approval. Trajectories that never approve in six cycles
are graded once at the end and never receive feedback; that grade is their
first pass, so those graded wrong count as first-pass wrong and
unrecovered.

On Omni-MATH the clause covers a minority of the declines of the
role-structured Reviewer, and in the free cycles it approves far less often than the
role-free team's third agent; gpt-oss is the closest of the three. On
SciBench the clause covers more. With at most one round of feedback the
role-free team recovers as much or more in every cell. Recoveries per
verdict after the first are higher with roles in every cell, but that ratio
compares different trajectories: most of the role-free team's later
verdicts are further attempts on problems it has already failed more than
once. The like-for-like comparison is the
verdict-matched one in the main paper (Table~2): on the problems both teams
first answer wrongly, with each role-free trajectory cut off after as many
verdicts as the role-structured team received on the same problem, no cell
shows a detectable difference. Section~\ref{sec:robust} tests how far that
comparison can be pushed.

\section{Robustness of the verdict-matched comparison}
\label{sec:robust}

Matching on verdicts is coarse. When the role-structured team received a
single verdict --- it never submitted and was graded at the end, or it
submitted once and stopped --- the role-free trajectory cut to one verdict
cannot recover either, and the pair ties at zero by construction. These
forced ties are most of the gemma-4 and nearly all of the Llama-3.3 pairs
(Table~\ref{tab:robust}), so the pooled interval of the main paper is
narrower than the information behind it. The cut is also one-sided: the
role-structured count stops at that team's own passing verdict, which can
only favor it. Table~\ref{tab:robust} therefore gives five variants: the
problems with at least two role-structured verdicts; both trajectories cut
at the smaller count; and the scoring defect recoded, each with and
without Llama-3.3. No variant puts the role-free team significantly ahead:
the two symmetric cuts favor it by $0.005$ and $0.010$, with intervals
that include zero. On the
informative problems the upper bounds reach $+0.065$ in favor of roles
($+0.073$ with the recoding), and with the recoding on all problems the role-structured team leads by $+0.029$ with an
interval that excludes zero. The defensible reading is the one the main
paper gives: matched on verdicts, the role-free advantage disappears, and
any per-verdict advantage of roles is small. Verdict matching equalizes
evaluator exposure, not calls: the role-structured team spends more cycles
per verdict.

\section{Removing the submission gate}
\label{sec:forced}

The forced-submission run tests the gate directly. It uses the
role-structured prompts and runner unchanged except for one step: after the
Reviewer's turn, the pipeline submits the team's current answer to the
evaluator whatever route the Reviewer chose, and the verdict and hint reach
the agents through the same role-specific slots as in the role-structured
condition. An offline test in the supplementary material (no model calls)
checks that the prompts are byte-identical to the role-structured ones up to
the first verdict and that the evaluator is called in every cycle. It was run
once, after the freeze, for gemma-4 on the $400$ Omni-MATH problems of the
role-structured re-run, with the same endpoint, budget and evaluator.
Table~\ref{tab:forced} compares it with that re-run, the frozen
role-structured cell and the role-free team with feedback, restricted to the
same problems. Recovery rises to the role-free team's level while first-pass
accuracy does not move; the Reviewer's logged approvals no longer decide
anything.

\section{Validation of the measures}
\label{sec:validation}

The definition was frozen before the adjudication sheet was opened; $72$
Planner messages were adjudicated by hand for \Pone{}, each with a recorded
reason. \Pone{} agrees with the hand labels on $0.903$ of messages (Cohen's
$\kappa=0.806$; precision $0.943$, recall $0.868$), clearing the pre-set
$0.80$ threshold. Agreement fell from $0.967$ at $n=30$ as adjudication
continued; we report the final figure. Its residual errors point in
opposite directions --- symbolic results with no digits are missed,
restated givens are flagged --- so they do not push the measure
consistently one way.

\section{An early campaign stopped for a truncation confound}
\label{sec:truncation}

The main run was gated on a smoke test requiring non-zero boundary
rejections with reasons logged, answer-bearing calls equal across
conditions, and a pinned backend. An early campaign was stopped after $121$
trajectories when $74\%$ of enforcement rejections proved to be the model
cut off at the token limit mid-JSON rather than violating a role boundary.
Truncation was not condition-specific but its \emph{cost} was --- a prose
condition tolerates a truncated message where the enforced one rejects
unparseable JSON --- so left alone it would have produced a convincing
``enforcement lowers accuracy'' artifact. The limit was doubled and the
pre-fix rows were never pooled.

\section{Structural enforcement: both scorings, and the attrition that
separates them}

The main paper reports that enforcement loosens the submission gate in all
three actors (and unlocks the answer for gemma-4 and Llama-3.3) but does
not become end-to-end accuracy, and that the apparent
accuracy benefit is survivorship. Table~\ref{tab:enforcement} is the evidence
for both halves: the manipulation check, the one-sided attrition, and the
same accuracy column under both scorings with intervals.

The enforced recovery and first-pass rates \textbf{do not compose} into
the accuracy column. They cannot: trajectories that never receive a first
verdict have no first pass, and those that die after one stay in the
recovery denominator, so the three quantities do not share a denominator
and the
$(1-\text{first-pass})\times\text{recovery}$ identity does not hold. The
pre-registration fixed in advance that unparseable trajectories count as
incorrect rather than being dropped, and that rule is what makes the
as-graded reading survivorship rather than a finding.

\paragraph{The context-restored control.} Restoring shared history at fixed
enforcement (gemma-4 and gpt-oss, $400$ problems each; attrition $4.5\%$ and
$9.8\%$) changes final accuracy relative to enforcement by $+0.0450$
$[+0.0125,+0.0775]$ ($p=0.0079$) for gemma-4 and by $-0.0125$
$[-0.0475,+0.0200]$ ($p=0.56$) for gpt-oss under the pre-registered scoring,
and by $+0.0196$ $[-0.0028,+0.0419]$ ($p=0.14$) and $-0.0089$
$[-0.0357,+0.0149]$ ($p=0.65$) as graded (exact McNemar, paired).

\section{The single-agent comparator}

Table~\ref{tab:singleagent} is the single-agent reference under the same
call cap. The main paper uses it as a guard against reading ``collapse
costs nothing'' as ``the system was one agent all along'', and reports both
directions.

The comparator comes from a \textbf{previously released corpus whose two
actor arms were produced in separate runs}. Each direction within
an actor is therefore a sound guard, and \emph{the gap between actors is
not}. The result cuts both ways: a collapsed gpt-oss team beats its own
single agent, while gemma-4's is significantly worse in both conditions.
Neither ``multi-agent systems are just single agents'' nor ``more agents
help'' survives contact with it.

\section{Accumulated context as a competing explanation}

Accumulated context is one candidate explanation of the role-structured
team's recovery advantage over the feedback-withheld team;
Table~\ref{tab:context} stratifies by it.

It is reported with a caveat that is not decoration. Quartile cut points
are computed over both conditions \emph{pooled}, and accumulated context is
nearly a function of condition --- the role-free team accumulates less
because it stops reworking. The strata are therefore not balanced, and this
comparison cannot separate context from condition. It is
\textbf{directional only}, and it is included because it does not behave
uniformly: the gap survives in the lower quartiles, but in gpt-oss's top
quartile the ordering reverses (as, on a handful of events, in
Llama-3.3's third).

\section{AI assistance}

Reproduced in full here rather than pointed at. AAMAS requires that
supplementary material ship inside the submission, and a disclosure a
reviewer cannot open is not a disclosure.

AAMAS asks that where AI-assisted technologies contribute to hypotheses,
methodology or experimental design, authors disclose the tool, version and
role. This work used them substantially in all three, so what follows is
detailed rather than pro forma.

\paragraph{Tools.} A large language model agent (Anthropic Claude,
Opus-class) performed research execution: experiment implementation,
campaign operation, analysis scripting, manuscript drafting and internal
review. Exact build identifiers were not retained for every session. A
frontier model was additionally used as a \emph{measurement instrument} for
the rubric-based adjudication of whether a division of labor was realized;
that model and rubric version are recorded and will be released with the
data after review. The
actor models under study are the experimental subjects, not assistance, and
are named in the paper.

\paragraph{What the agents did.} The hypotheses tested here --- whether
role organization contributes to first-pass generation and to post-failure
revision, and through which channel --- were formed with the agents'
assistance and then subjected to pre-registered and exploratory tests. The
pre-registered primary hypothesis (P4) was not supported, and the paper
reports that null. Condition design,
budget matching, the pre-registration and the stopping and exclusion rules
were drafted by agents under human direction and frozen before confirmatory
analysis. Agents wrote the runner, operated and froze the campaign, wrote
all analysis code, drafted and revised the manuscript, and conducted
several independent internal reviews --- including blind reviews given only
the compiled PDF, and adversarial audits tasked with invalidating the
paper's own claims. Every table is generated by script from hash-manifested
data (the frozen snapshot or the separately manifested post-freeze runs;
the single-agent comparator excepted), and every number in the prose was
checked against the scripts' outputs.

\paragraph{What the human authors directed and approved.} Research direction
and every pivot; the decision to pre-register and the pre-declared
not-submit conditions; the choice of actor models and domains; the decision
to use model adjudication in place of human annotation, with the
instruction not to overstate what it establishes; the instruction that the
page limit may not be met by hiding caveats; and final approval of all
claims. The scope limits --- not overstating actor generality, not
publishing the data during review --- were human decisions.

\paragraph{Prompts, and what was not retained.} The exact role prompts,
schemas and evaluator prompt are included in the supplementary material. Session prompts directing the agents' research work were
\textbf{not retained verbatim}, and we do not reconstruct prompts that were
not kept; standing instructions are reconstructed in the project record
from the decision log. Model build identifiers were not retained for every
session. Everything load-bearing for a reported number --- experimental
prompts, schemas, configurations, budgets, and the frozen rows themselves
--- is retained; the code and manifests are in the supplementary material,
and the trajectories will be released after review.

\paragraph{Defects disclosed in the paper.} Agent review of the
instrumentation and analysis surfaced three properties of the data that the
paper reports rather than repairs: a logging defect that left one actor's
planner output unrecorded; a scoring defect that inflates the main
contrast, reported with a recoded sensitivity analysis; and a renamed role
token visible to the adjudicator, which is why the paper describes its
adjudication as outcome-blind but not condition-blind.
