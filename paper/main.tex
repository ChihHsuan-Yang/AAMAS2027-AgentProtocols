\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent large language model (LLM) systems usually assign roles such as Planner, Executor and
Reviewer. We ask when these roles are load-bearing, that is, when removing
them lowers performance while call and information budgets stay the same. We
test three open-weight models on competition mathematics and two of them on
college science. Keeping the agents, topology and budgets unchanged, we
delete every role instruction. This deletion largely destroys the intended
division of labor, yet first-pass accuracy does not drop. Roles appear useful for repairing rejected answers, clearly for the gemma-4
model and directionally for the gpt-oss model. But this advantage appears only when the role-free team never
receives the evaluator's feedback. In an exploratory condition, we give the
role-free team the same feedback string. It then recovers from failures more
often than the role-structured team (recovery $0.609$ against $0.222$ for
gemma-4 on competition mathematics) and reaches higher final accuracy in
every setting. However, the advantage disappears when both teams are
credited with the same number of evaluator verdicts, and one robustness
check even favors roles. The difference in recovery mainly reflects how
often each team submits. The role-structured Reviewer often withholds
approval, so its team receives fewer verdicts. In a single exploratory
$400$-problem run for gemma-4, we submitted the team's answer in every cycle (one pass of the three agents) regardless of the Reviewer, leaving the role prompts unchanged. The
role-structured team then recovered as well as the role-free team. We
conclude that a clean division of labor is not evidence of adaptability.
Role ablations must hold information channels fixed, not only budgets.
\end{abstract}
