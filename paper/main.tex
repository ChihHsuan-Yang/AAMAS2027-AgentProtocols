\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent LLM systems are specified as role diagrams -- Planner, Executor,
Reviewer -- and those diagrams are read as mechanism: an account of where the
system's competence comes from. We ask when that organization is actually
\emph{load-bearing}, defining the term operationally: a component is
load-bearing if removing or altering it causes a reproducible performance
degradation under matched compute and information budgets. Holding agents, call
budget, context policy, verifier and grading fixed, we delete all role
instructions (Arm~B) and, separately, make the division of labor structurally
unrepresentable (Arm~C), across three actor models on 9{,}079 matched
trajectories.

Deleting role instructions destroys the functional division of labor in
\emph{every} actor tested: the rate at which all three agents independently
assert their own final answer rises to $0.846$--$0.975$, and a blinded,
rubric-frozen adjudication agrees -- including on a cell where an
exact-duplication metric reports zero. What differs is what it costs. One actor
loses accuracy ($-0.030$ $[-0.046,-0.014]$) and another does not ($+0.008$
$[-0.011,+0.026]$). \textbf{How much organization an actor loses does not
predict whether losing it is costly.} This is not degeneration to a single
agent: a collapsed team still beats its budget-matched single agent
($+0.025$ $[+0.007,+0.042]$). Architectural enforcement completes the picture
from the other side, driving answer-authorship compliance to $1.000$ while
moving accuracy for neither actor -- our pre-registered primary test is null and
we report it as such. A mechanism analysis locates the difference: role removal
leaves the vulnerable actor's first-pass solve rate unchanged and destroys its
\emph{recovery}, because without roles the team reaches near-total unanimity and
cannot escape a wrong consensus. Visible organization is not evidence about the
mechanism carrying performance.
\end{abstract}
