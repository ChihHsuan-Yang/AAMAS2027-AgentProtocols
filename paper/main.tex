\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent LLM systems are specified as role diagrams -- Planner, Executor,
Reviewer -- and those diagrams are read as mechanism: an account of where the
system's competence comes from. We ask when that organization is actually
\emph{load-bearing}, defining the term operationally: a component is
load-bearing if removing or altering it causes a reproducible performance
degradation under matched compute and information budgets. Holding agents,
call budget, context policy, verifier, and grading fixed, we delete all role
instructions (Arm~B) and, separately, make the division of labor structurally
unrepresentable (Arm~C), across two actor models on 8{,}490 matched
trajectories. Deleting role instructions collapses the organization of the
\emph{more} capable actor almost completely -- $69.8\%$ of first pipeline
passes become byte-identical agent outputs -- at no accuracy cost, including
on the hardest problems, where collapse is most frequent. The less capable
actor shows \emph{no} first-pass collapse and loses accuracy anyway. Visible
organization and performance are thus dissociated in both directions. This
is not degeneration to a single agent: the collapsed team still beats its
budget-matched single agent by $+0.0248$ $[+0.0072,+0.0424]$. Architectural
enforcement completes the picture from the other side, driving
answer-authorship compliance to $1.000$ and collapse to ${\approx}0$ while
moving accuracy for neither actor -- our pre-registered primary test is null,
and we report it as such. A mechanism analysis locates the difference: role
removal leaves the vulnerable actor's first-pass solve rate unchanged
($0.809 \rightarrow 0.809$) and destroys its recovery ($0.490 \rightarrow
0.045$), because without roles the team reaches near-total unanimity and
cannot escape a wrong consensus. Multi-agent computation and differentiated
organization are separable, and which one is load-bearing is
actor-dependent -- a sign inversion that monotone capability accounts, under
which structure helps weaker models, do not predict.
\end{abstract}
