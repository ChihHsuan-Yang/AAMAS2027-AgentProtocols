\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent LLM systems are commonly organized by assigning agents roles --
Planner, Executor, Reviewer -- and describing each role's function in natural
language. Whether those descriptions produce a functional division of labor,
and whether that depends on which model fills the roles, is rarely measured.
We separate two constructs that are usually conflated. \emph{Role
distinguishability} asks whether an observer can recover an agent's role from
its behavior alone; \emph{role fidelity} asks whether an agent performs the
function its contract assigns, scored clause-by-clause against the contract
that agent actually received. Across 145{,}411 role messages from matched
Planner--Executor--Reviewer trajectories, the two dissociate. Roles are highly
distinguishable (0.90 balanced accuracy from behavioral features), but that
separability rests on output formatting and answer commitment rather than on
the cognitive division the contract specifies: restricted to computation
behavior, role separability falls to 0.71, barely above a message-length
control. Fidelity to individual clauses of one contract ranges from 0.96 to
0.04, and differs sharply across actor models given byte-identical prompts.
We validate these measures against human annotation and report where automated
proxies fail. We then ask whether enforcing the division of labor
\emph{architecturally} -- through interfaces that make contract violation
unrepresentable, rather than through stronger prompting -- changes the
effectiveness of the same organization, and whether that effect depends on the
actor model. \TODO{final sentence: replace with the outcome once Arms A/B/C
report; keep as a question if they do not}
\end{abstract}
